# Tourism & Hospitality Management Paper Writing

## Overview

Tourism and hospitality management papers span a uniquely broad methodological spectrum — from econometric modeling of tourism demand to ethnographic studies of host-guest interactions, from structural equation modeling of destination image to netnographic analysis of online travel communities. The pipeline follows: **Phenomenon → Theoretical Lens → Research Design → Data → Analysis → Implications for Destination/Hospitality Management**.

This skill provides journal-specific guidance for 6 flagship tourism and hospitality journals across two tiers, covering the full paper lifecycle.

**Core Principle**: A tourism paper must contribute to **both theory and practice**. It should advance academic understanding of tourism as a social and economic phenomenon while providing actionable guidance for destination managers, hotel operators, policymakers, and community stakeholders.

---

## When to Use This Skill

- Drafting a paper targeting TM, ATR, JTR, IJHM, IJCHM, JST, or any tourism/hospitality journal
- Deciding which journal best fits a particular phenomenon-method-contribution combination
- Designing a research study: tourism demand modeling, SEM-based tourist behavior, discrete choice experiments, qualitative ethnography, or mixed-methods
- Developing a theoretical framework grounded in tourism/hospitality literature
- Writing up survey-based research with established tourism measurement scales
- Structuring a discussion section that links findings to theory and practice
- Revising based on reviewer feedback from a tourism/hospitality journal
- Converting a working paper or thesis chapter between tourism journal formats

## Journal Selection Quick Reference

Choosing the right journal is a function of your paper's **theoretical depth**, **methodological approach**, **phenomenon focus**, and **practical relevance**.

### Tier 1: Top Flagship Tourism/Hospitality Journals

| Journal | Core Identity | Ideal Paper Profile |
|---------|--------------|---------------------|
| **TM** (Tourism Management) | Broad tourism management, quantitative emphasis, managerial relevance | Strong empirical study with rigorous quantitative or mixed-methods design. Clear destination/hospitality management implications. Large and justified samples. Length: ~8,000 words (max 9,000). |
| **ATR** (Annals of Tourism Research) | Social science of tourism, theory-driven, interdisciplinary | Theoretical contribution is paramount. Welcomes qualitative, quantitative, and conceptual papers. Must engage with social science discourse — not merely apply theory but advance it. Length: ~9,000 words (max 10,000). |
| **JTR** (Journal of Travel Research) | Traveler behavior, destination marketing, consumer psychology | Consumer/tourist behavior focus. Strong experimental or survey design. Quantitative methods preferred. Emphasis on marketing implications for destinations. Length: ~8,000 words (max 9,000). |

### Tier 2: Strong Field Journals

| Journal | Core Identity | Ideal Paper Profile |
|---------|--------------|---------------------|
| **IJHM** (Int. J. Hospitality Management) | Hospitality operations, HRM, service quality, food & beverage | Hospitality-industry specific. Covers operational management, human resource management, service encounter, revenue management, and foodservice. Strong empirical emphasis, preferably with industry data. Length: ~7,000 words. |
| **IJCHM** (Int. J. Contemporary Hospitality Mgmt) | Contemporary issues, practitioner-oriented, trend-responsive | Addresses current and emerging hospitality issues. Practitioner relevance is critical. Welcomes review papers, case studies, and trend analyses. Must be timely and forward-looking. Length: ~7,000 words. |
| **JST** (Journal of Sustainable Tourism) | Sustainability, ecotourism, community impact, climate change | Sustainability-focused across all tourism contexts. Covers environmental, economic, and socio-cultural dimensions. Values community-based and participatory research. Strong on policy implications. Length: ~8,000 words (max 9,000). |

### Decision Flowchart

```
What is the primary nature of your contribution?
│
├── THEORETICAL advance in understanding tourism as a social phenomenon
│   └── ATR (Annals of Tourism Research)
│
├── MANAGERIAL/PRACTICAL implications for tourism destinations or businesses
│   ├── Quantitative or mixed-methods empirical study
│   │   ├── Focus on tourist/consumer behavior → JTR
│   │   ├── Focus on destination management or broad tourism → TM
│   │   └── Focus on hospitality operations/HR/service → IJHM
│   └── Qualitative or conceptual study
│       ├── Strong social science theory → ATR
│       └── Contemporary/trend-focused → IJCHM
│
└── SUSTAINABILITY focus (environmental, social, economic)
    └── JST (Journal of Sustainable Tourism)

Secondary considerations:
- Is your study HOSPITALITY-specific (hotels, restaurants, events)?
  ├── YES → IJHM or IJCHM
  └── NO → TM, ATR, JTR, or JST
- Is your study CONTEMPORARY/TREND-driven (e.g., sharing economy, AI in tourism, COVID recovery)?
  ├── YES → IJCHM tends to be more receptive
  └── NO → TM, ATR, JTR
- Does your study use QUALITATIVE methods exclusively?
  ├── YES and strong theory → ATR
  ├── YES and sustainability → JST
  └── YES and contemporary issue → IJCHM
```

## The Tourism Paper Structure

Unlike the IMRAD format in biomedical sciences or the model-analysis-algorithm structure in MS&E, tourism papers follow a modified IMRAD structure with strong emphasis on theoretical framing and practical implications:

| Section | Typical Pages | Key Content |
|---------|--------------|-------------|
| Abstract | 150-250 words | Tourism phenomenon, theoretical lens, methodology, key findings, theoretical + practical implications |
| S1 Introduction | 2-3 pages | Tourism phenomenon hook → Research gap in tourism/hospitality literature → Theoretical lens positioning → Statement of purpose → Numbered contributions → Paper organization |
| S2 Literature Review & Theoretical Framework | 4-6 pages | Organized by conceptual streams (not paper-by-paper) → Hypotheses development (if quantitative) OR conceptual model (if qualitative) → Explicit differentiation table |
| S3 Methodology | 3-5 pages | Study context (destination/hotel/event), research design, sampling strategy, measurement instruments (with sources), data collection procedure, analytical approach (with justification), common method bias remedies |
| S4 Results | 3-6 pages | Sample profile/descriptive statistics, measurement model (CFA, reliability, validity), structural model / hypothesis tests, robustness checks, effect sizes |
| S5 Discussion & Implications | 3-5 pages | Summary of findings → Theoretical contributions (connecting back to §2) → Practical implications for destination/hospitality managers → Policy implications (if applicable) → Limitations and future research |
| S6 Conclusion | 0.5-1 page | Concise synthesis of the study's contribution and significance |
| References | — | 50-80 papers typical for tourism journals |
| Appendix | As needed | Survey instruments, additional robustness checks, supplementary analyses |

**Key structural difference from management science papers**: The **Literature Review & Theoretical Framework section is the backbone**. Hypotheses or research propositions must be clearly grounded in prior tourism/hospitality theory. Reviewers evaluate whether your conceptual model flows logically from the literature before assessing your methodology.

## Methodology Guide by Approach

See [references/quantitative-methods.md](references/quantitative-methods.md) and [references/qualitative-methods.md](references/qualitative-methods.md) for comprehensive guidance.

### A. Quantitative Methods

#### A1. Tourism Demand Modeling

Tourism demand modeling is a major stream in TM and JTR. Standard approaches:

**Time-Series Approaches**:
- ARIMA / SARIMA for modeling seasonality in tourist arrivals
- VAR (Vector Autoregression) for multi-destination demand systems
- ADLM (Autoregressive Distributed Lag Models) for long-run elasticity estimation
- Error correction models (ECM) for short-run dynamics and long-run equilibrium
- GARCH-family models for modeling tourism demand volatility

**Key Variables** (standard in tourism demand literature):
- Economic: GDP per capita (origin), relative prices, exchange rates, transport costs
- Seasonality: monthly/quarterly dummies, climate variables
- Shocks: financial crises, pandemics (SARS, COVID-19), terrorism events, natural disasters
- Policy: visa restrictions, tourism taxes, aviation liberalization
- Marketing: tourism promotion expenditure, destination branding campaigns

**Panel Data Approaches**:
- Fixed effects / random effects for multi-destination panels
- Dynamic panel GMM (Arellano-Bond, Blundell-Bond) to handle persistence in tourism flows
- Gravity models for bilateral tourism flows (origin-destination pairs)
- Spatial econometric models for spatial dependence among destinations

**Data Sources**: UNWTO Tourism Statistics, national tourism offices, IATA, STR Global, Google Trends, TripAdvisor/online review platforms.

#### A2. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM)

SEM is the dominant quantitative method in tourism/hospitality research, especially in TM, JTR, and IJHM.

**Covariance-Based SEM (CB-SEM)**:
- Software: AMOS, LISREL, Mplus
- Preferred by: TM, ATR (for theory testing)
- Requirements: multivariate normality, sample size > 200 (ideally > 10:1 observations-to-parameters ratio)
- Reporting: χ², df, χ²/df (< 3), CFI (> 0.90, ideally > 0.95), TLI (> 0.90), RMSEA (< 0.08, ideally < 0.06), SRMR (< 0.08)

**Partial Least Squares SEM (PLS-SEM)**:
- Software: SmartPLS, WarpPLS, R (seminr, plspm)
- Preferred by: IJHM, IJCHM (for prediction-oriented studies)
- Requirements: fewer distributional assumptions, handles formative constructs, smaller samples acceptable
- Reporting: path coefficients (β), t-values, p-values, R², Q² (predictive relevance), f² (effect size), HTMT (discriminant validity)

**Common Analytical Steps** for SEM papers:
1. Data screening: missing values, outliers, normality (skewness/kurtosis)
2. Common method bias assessment: Harman's single-factor test, marker variable technique, procedural remedies
3. Measurement model: CFA, factor loadings (> 0.70), composite reliability (> 0.70), AVE (> 0.50), discriminant validity (Fornell-Larcker or HTMT)
4. Structural model: path coefficients, R², predictive relevance (Q²), effect sizes (f²)
5. Mediation analysis: bootstrapped indirect effects with bias-corrected confidence intervals (Preacher & Hayes, 2008)
6. Moderation / multi-group analysis: interaction effects, group comparisons (e.g., first-time vs. repeat visitors)
7. Robustness: alternative model specifications, endogeneity checks, non-response bias assessment

#### A3. Discrete Choice Experiments (DCE)

Growing in tourism research for destination choice, hotel selection, and activity participation modeling.

**Approach**:
- Multinomial logit (MNL): baseline model, IIA assumption
- Mixed logit (random parameters logit): relaxes IIA, captures preference heterogeneity
- Latent class model: identifies tourist segments with distinct preferences
- Nested logit: for hierarchical choice structures (destination → hotel → room type)

**Attribute Selection**: Price, distance, environmental quality, cultural attractions, accommodation type, season.
**Design**: Orthogonal or efficient designs using Ngene or SAS; fractional factorial designs for large attribute sets.
**Sample**: Stated preference data collected via structured surveys; typically 300-800 respondents.

#### A4. Additional Quantitative Methods

- **Conjoint analysis**: Destination attribute trade-offs; importance-utility estimation
- **Experimental design**: Vignette-based scenario experiments; field experiments in hospitality settings
- **Big data analytics**: Sentiment analysis of online reviews (TripAdvisor, Booking.com); GPS tracking of tourist spatial behavior; social media content analysis (Instagram, Twitter)
- **Machine learning**: Random forest / XGBoost for tourism demand forecasting; LDA for topic modeling of tourist reviews
- **Importance-Performance Analysis (IPA)**: Destination attribute evaluation; service quality assessment
- **Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA)**: Hotel efficiency; destination competitiveness benchmarking

### B. Qualitative Methods

#### B1. Grounded Theory

A primary method in ATR for inductive theory building from tourist/host/manager experiences.

**Approaches**:
- Glaserian (classic): emergence, no preconceived framework
- Straussian (systematic): open → axial → selective coding
- Constructivist (Charmaz): co-construction of meaning, reflexive

**Application**: Understanding novel tourism phenomena where existing theory is insufficient (e.g., Airbnb host-guest relationships, transformative travel experiences, dark tourism motivations).

**Reporting Standards**: Describe coding process, theoretical sampling logic, saturation point, member checking procedures.

#### B2. Ethnography

Ethnographic methods are valued in ATR and JST for deep understanding of tourism communities and host-guest encounters.

**Approaches**:
- Traditional ethnography: extended participant observation in tourism communities
- Focused ethnography: shorter, targeted observations of specific tourism settings
- Auto-ethnography: researcher's own tourism experience as data
- **Netnography** (Kozinets): online community observation — travel forums, TripAdvisor communities, Instagram travel influencers

**Reporting**: Thick description (Geertz), positionality statement, temporal and spatial context, triangulation of observations with interviews and documents.

#### B3. Phenomenology

Understanding the lived experience of tourists, hosts, or hospitality employees.

**Approaches**:
- Descriptive (Husserlian): bracketing, essence of experience
- Interpretive (Heideggerian): being-in-the-world, Dasein, hermeneutic circle
- IPA (Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis): idiographic, double hermeneutic

**Application**: Memorable tourism experiences, spiritual/wellness tourism, volunteer tourism motivations, hospitality emotional labor.

#### B4. Case Study Research

**Design** (Yin, 2018):
- Single case: critical, extreme, revelatory, or longitudinal case
- Multiple case: literal replication (similar results) or theoretical replication (contrasting results)
- Embedded vs. holistic unit of analysis

**Application**: Destination governance, tourism policy implementation, hotel management practices, crisis management in tourism.

**Reporting**: Case selection justification, case description, within-case analysis, cross-case synthesis, pattern matching.

#### B5. Content Analysis

**Qualitative content analysis**:
- Thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006): familiarization → coding → theme generation → review → definition → write-up
- Directed content analysis: theory-driven coding framework
- Summative content analysis: counts and contextual interpretation

**Application**: Destination image from travel blogs, sustainability reporting by tourism firms, policy document analysis, tourist online review analysis.

### C. Mixed Methods

Mixed-methods research is increasingly demanded and valued, particularly in ATR and JST.

| Design | Sequence | Purpose | Example in Tourism |
|--------|----------|---------|-------------------|
| **Sequential Explanatory** | QUAN → qual | Qualitative data explains quantitative results | Survey (TM image + satisfaction) → follow-up interviews to understand WHY certain relationships emerged |
| **Sequential Exploratory** | QUAL → quan | Qualitative exploration generates hypotheses for quantitative testing | Interviews exploring tourist experience → develop survey instrument → quantitative test of model |
| **Convergent Parallel** | QUAL + QUAN simultaneous | Triangulation and complementarity | Survey on resident attitudes toward tourism + focus groups with community leaders, collected concurrently |
| **Embedded** | QUAN(qual) or QUAL(quan) | One method is primary, the other supportive | Large-scale hotel guest survey with embedded open-ended questions |

**Reporting Standards (GRADE-CERQual for qualitative synthesis)**:
- Explicitly describe integration point (methods, interpretation, or reporting level)
- Use joint displays to present integrated findings
- Address divergence between qualitative and quantitative results (not just convergence)
- Follow mixed-methods reporting guidelines (e.g., Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study — GRAMMS)

## Common Rejection Reasons in Tourism Journals

| Reason | Frequency | Prevention |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| Descriptive only — no theoretical contribution or theoretical grounding is superficial | Very High | Ground every hypothesis/proposition in established theory from tourism, psychology, sociology, or marketing. ATR is particularly stringent on this. |
| Common method bias (single-source, self-report, cross-sectional survey) | Very High | Multi-source data (tourists + managers + secondary); temporal separation; procedural remedies (anonymity, item order); statistical remedies (marker variable, CFA marker technique); acknowledge as limitation if unavoidable |
| Weak measurement — unvalidated or poorly adapted scales with no psychometric evidence reported | High | Use established scales. Report EFA and/or CFA results. Report α (> 0.70), CR (> 0.70), AVE (> 0.50), and discriminant validity. For new scales, follow scale development procedures (DeVellis; Hinkin). |
| Convenience sample with no justification — student samples, single-destination samples | High | Justify sampling strategy theoretically. Discuss boundary conditions. Consider multi-destination or multi-stakeholder sampling. Acknowledge generalizability limitations honestly. |
| Sample size too small for the analytical method | Medium | SEM: N > 200 (or 10:1 ratio); regression: power analysis; qualitative: describe saturation point. State how sample size was determined. |
| Hypotheses not grounded in theory — reads like a fishing expedition | High | Each hypothesis must have a clear theoretical rationale paragraph before it is stated. Avoid long chains of "H1 → H2 → H3" without conceptual logic between them. |
| Managerial/practical implications too generic ("managers should improve service quality") | High | Provide specific, context-grounded, actionable recommendations. Name specific tourism contexts. Distinguish implications for different stakeholders (DMOs, hotel managers, policymakers). |
| Cross-sectional design used to infer causality — no temporal precedence | Medium | Use experiments (vignette-based, field), longitudinal designs, or at minimum acknowledge this limitation explicitly. Use temporal language carefully (avoid "leads to," prefer "is associated with"). |
| Limited contribution over existing literature — paper is incremental | High | Explicit gap table comparing 8-15 key references across theoretical, methodological, and contextual dimensions. Show exactly what your paper adds that prior work does not. |
| Theoretical framework is a "laundry list" of variables with no coherent narrative | Medium | Use a single theoretical lens or carefully integrate 2-3 complementary theories. Diagram the conceptual model. Explain the nomological network linking constructs. |
| Poor fit with the journal's scope | Medium | Use the journal selection guide above. Read the journal's aims and scope carefully. Check recent issues to see if similar papers appear. |
| Qualitative paper lacks rigor indicators | Medium | Report: prolonged engagement, triangulation, member checking, peer debriefing, negative case analysis, audit trail, thick description, reflexivity (Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Korstjens & Moser, 2018). |
| Endogeneity not addressed in econometric studies | Medium | Tourism demand models must address endogeneity (instrumental variables, GMM, control function, propensity score matching). |
| Non-response bias not assessed | Low-Medium | Compare early vs. late respondents on key variables. Report response rate and any known differences between respondents and non-respondents. |

## 0-to-Draft Pipeline: Five-Stage Workflow

This is the core workflow of this skill. Each stage builds on the previous. Stages 1-3 are sequential; Stages 4-5 can partially overlap. At each stage, ask the user for their current materials before proceeding.

---

### Stage 1: Phenomenon & Positioning

**Goal**: Identify the tourism/hospitality phenomenon, select a theoretical lens, define the contribution gap, choose a target journal, and establish the writing plan.

**Input needed from user**: Research area, tourism/hospitality context, preliminary phenomenon of interest, any existing notes or literature.

**Agent actions**:

```
1.1 Understand the tourism/hospitality phenomenon
    - Ask: What is the specific tourism or hospitality phenomenon? In what context (destination, hotel, event, community)?
    - Identify: Is this about tourist behavior? Destination management? Hospitality operations? Sustainability? Host-guest relations?
    - Classify: Phenomenon → existing explanations in literature → what remains unexplained?
    - Output: one-paragraph phenomenon statement with context

1.2 Theoretical lens selection
    - Based on the phenomenon, identify candidate theoretical lenses:
        - Tourist behavior: Theory of Planned Behavior (Ajzen), S-O-R framework, Push-Pull theory (Crompton, Dann), Self-Determination Theory
        - Destination: Destination Competitiveness (Ritchie & Crouch), Tourism Area Life Cycle (Butler), Social Exchange Theory (for resident attitudes)
        - Hospitality: Service-Dominant Logic, Social Exchange Theory, Emotional Labor (Hochschild), Job Demands-Resources
        - Experience: Experience Economy (Pine & Gilmore), Memorable Tourism Experience (Kim), Authenticity theory (Wang, MacCannell)
        - Sustainability: Triple Bottom Line, Social-Ecological Systems, Community-Based Tourism frameworks
    - Evaluate fit: Does the theory explain the mechanism? Can it generate testable hypotheses?
    - Output: recommended theoretical lens with rationale + 1-2 alternatives

1.3 Literature gap analysis
    - Search for 10-15 closest papers using research-lookup or paper-lookup
    - Map literature along 3-4 dimensions relevant to your study (e.g., theory used, method, context/destination type, stakeholder group, constructs examined)
    - Build a Gap Table: rows = key references, columns = dimensions, your paper = last row with empty cells filled
    - Identify the specific gap: theoretical (new lens applied), methodological (new method), contextual (new destination/hospitality type), or construct-level (new mediator/moderator)
    - Output: draft gap table with explicit differentiation

1.4 Journal selection
    - Use the Journal Selection Quick Reference and Decision Flowchart above
    - Match paper profile (phenomenon, method, theoretical depth, practical relevance) to journal identity
    - Consider: ATR (theory-first) vs. TM (management-first) vs. JTR (consumer behavior-first) vs. JST (sustainability-first)
    - Output: recommended primary journal + 1-2 fallback options with rationale

1.5 Contribution statement
    - Formulate the one-sentence contribution: "Drawing on [theory], this study examines [phenomenon] in [context] using [method], and finds that [key insight], contributing to [theoretical domain] and offering guidance for [practitioner group]."
    - Draft 3-4 numbered contributions: theoretical (1-2), methodological (0-1), practical (1-2)
    - Output: contribution list

1.6 Writing plan
    - Outline the paper structure with estimated page allocation per section based on target journal
    - Identify which reference files to load at each stage
    - Identify figures needed: conceptual model diagram, study context map, results summary figure
    - Output: section-by-section writing plan with word/page targets
```

**Stage 1 exit criteria**: Tourism phenomenon clearly articulated, theoretical lens selected, gap table drafted, target journal chosen, contribution list written, writing plan ready.

**Reference to load**: `references/journal-characteristics.md` (for chosen journal)

---

### Stage 2: Research Design

**Goal**: Design the research methodology — sampling strategy, measurement instruments, data collection procedure, and analytical approach.

**Input needed from user**: Research phenomenon and theoretical lens (from Stage 1), preferred methodological orientation, access to study context.

**Agent actions**:

```
2.1 Research paradigm and approach selection
    - Determine: Positivist (quantitative), Interpretivist (qualitative), or Pragmatist (mixed methods)?
    - Align with the target journal's methodological preferences:
        - TM: quantitative or mixed methods; pure qualitative is rare
        - ATR: all paradigms accepted; qualitative and conceptual are common
        - JTR: quantitative strongly preferred; experiments valued
        - IJHM: quantitative empirical; case studies accepted
        - IJCHM: mixed methods; practitioner data valued
        - JST: all paradigms; community-based participatory research valued
    - Output: research paradigm + overall approach justification

2.2 Sampling strategy design
    - Define population of interest (international tourists? domestic visitors? hotel employees? residents? destination managers?)
    - Sampling approach:
        - Probability: simple random, stratified (by nationality, hotel star rating), cluster (by destinations)
        - Non-probability: purposive (qualitative), quota, convenience (with justification and limitations), snowball (for hard-to-reach populations)
    - Sample size determination:
        - Quantitative: power analysis (G*Power), rules of thumb (SEM: N > 200 or 10:1; regression: N > 50 + 8*m where m = predictors)
        - Qualitative: information power (Malterud et al., 2016), concept of saturation (Guest et al., 2006; Hennink & Kaiser, 2022)
    - Output: sampling strategy section draft + sample size justification

2.3 Measurement instrument development/selection
    - For each construct in the conceptual model:
        - Identify established scale from tourism/hospitality literature (see Measurement Scales table)
        - If adapting: justify modifications; report face validity assessment (expert panel)
        - If developing new: follow scale development procedures (item generation → expert review → pilot test → EFA → CFA)
    - Survey structure (if quantitative):
        - Screening questions
        - Construct items (Likert 5- or 7-point; semantic differential for image)
        - Travel behavior/characteristics
        - Demographics
    - Interview/focus group protocol (if qualitative):
        - Semi-structured question guide with prompts
        - Grand tour questions → follow-up probes
    - Output: measurement instrument draft with sources for each construct

2.4 Data collection procedure
    - Mode: face-to-face intercept (tourist sites, airports, hotels), online panel (e.g., MTurk, Prolific — controversial in tourism), mail survey, telephone
    - Timing: seasonal considerations (peak vs. off-peak), day of week, time of day
    - Context: specific destination, hotel chain, event, online community
    - Procedure: screening → consent → survey/interview → debrief → incentive
    - Common method bias procedural remedies: temporal separation, proximal separation, anonymity assurance, item order randomization
    - Output: data collection procedure section draft

2.5 Analytical approach specification
    - Quantitative: specify software (SPSS, AMOS, Mplus, Stata, R), analytical steps (data screening → measurement model → structural model → mediation/moderation → robustness)
    - Qualitative: specify approach (thematic analysis, grounded theory coding, content analysis), software (NVivo, MAXQDA, ATLAS.ti), coding framework
    - Mixed methods: specify integration point and joint display format
    - Output: analytical approach section draft

2.6 Ethics and quality assurance
    - Ethics: IRB/ethics committee approval, informed consent, anonymity, data protection (GDPR considerations for European tourism studies)
    - Quality (quantitative): pilot testing, back-translation for multi-language surveys, attention checks
    - Quality (qualitative): credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability (Lincoln & Guba)
    - Output: ethics and quality assurance paragraph
```

**Stage 2 exit criteria**: Complete methodology section draft with research design, sampling, measures, procedure, and analytical approach specified and justified.

**References to load**: `references/quantitative-methods.md` (if quantitative), `references/qualitative-methods.md` (if qualitative), `references/measurement-scales.md`

---

### Stage 3: Data Collection & Analysis

**Goal**: Conduct data collection (or access secondary data), perform analysis, and draft results section.

**Input needed from user**: Methodology design (from Stage 2), collected data or access to secondary data, analysis software.

**Agent actions**:

```
3.1 Data preparation (quantitative)
    - Data screening protocol:
        - Missing data: pattern analysis (MCAR, MAR, MNAR); handling (listwise, pairwise, imputation, FIML)
        - Outliers: univariate (z-scores > |3.29|) and multivariate (Mahalanobis D²)
        - Normality: skewness (|sk| < 2) and kurtosis (|ku| < 7) for SEM
        - Multicollinearity: VIF < 10 (ideally < 5), tolerance > 0.10
        - Linearity: scatterplot inspection
    - Data transformation if needed (log, square root, Box-Cox)
    - Output: data screening report with decisions and justifications

3.2 Sample profile and descriptive statistics
    - Respondent profile table: demographics (age, gender, nationality, education, income), travel characteristics (purpose, party size, length of stay, repeat vs. first-time)
    - Construct descriptive statistics: mean, SD, skewness, kurtosis for each item and construct
    - Correlation matrix with square root of AVE on diagonal (Fornell-Larcker)
    - Output: Tables 1-3 (sample profile, descriptives, correlations)

3.3 Common method bias assessment (if cross-sectional survey)
    - Harman's single-factor test: unrotated EFA; if single factor < 50% variance, CMB is not major concern
    - Marker variable technique (Lindell & Whitney, 2001): partial correlation adjustment
    - CFA marker technique (Williams et al., 2010)
    - Acknowledge limitations even after remedies
    - Output: CMB assessment paragraph

3.4 Measurement model (if SEM)
    - Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA):
        - Model fit: χ²/df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA [90% CI], SRMR
        - Factor loadings: all > 0.50, ideally > 0.70
        - Composite Reliability (CR): > 0.70 for each construct
        - Average Variance Extracted (AVE): > 0.50 for each construct
        - Discriminant validity: Fornell-Larcker criterion (√AVE > inter-construct correlations) AND/OR HTMT < 0.85 (or < 0.90 for conceptually similar constructs)
        - If problems: item deletion (report and justify), error covariance (with theoretical rationale)
    - Output: CFA results table + measurement model quality assessment

3.5 Structural model / hypothesis testing
    - For SEM: standardized path coefficients (β), standard errors, t-values, p-values, R², Q²
    - For regression: unstandardized (B) and standardized (β) coefficients, SE, t, p, R², adjusted R², ΔR²
    - For tourism demand models: coefficient estimates, elasticities (short-run, long-run), diagnostic tests (serial correlation, heteroskedasticity)
    - For discrete choice: parameter estimates, WTP, odds ratios, marginal effects
    - For experiments: ANOVA/ANCOVA, mean comparisons, effect sizes (η², Cohen's d)
    - Report effect sizes: f² for SEM, Cohen's d for group differences
    - Output: hypothesis testing summary table + structural model results

3.6 Mediation and moderation analysis
    - Mediation: bootstrapped indirect effects (5,000 or 10,000 resamples), bias-corrected 95% CI (Preacher & Hayes)
    - Moderation: interaction plots (±1 SD), simple slope analysis (Aiken & West)
    - Moderated mediation (conditional indirect effects) if applicable
    - Output: mediation/moderation results with tables and interaction plots

3.7 Robustness checks
    - Endogeneity: instrumental variable approach (2SLS), Gaussian copula method, or at minimum acknowledge
    - Non-response bias: compare early vs. late respondents on key constructs
    - Alternative model comparison: compare hypothesized model against plausible alternatives (Δχ², ΔCFI)
    - Common method variance: marker variable adjustment
    - Heterogeneity: multi-group analysis by key demographics or travel characteristics
    - Output: robustness checks section

3.8 Qualitative data analysis
    - Coding process documentation:
        - First cycle: initial/open coding, in vivo coding
        - Second cycle: axial/thematic/focused coding
        - Third cycle (if applicable): theoretical coding
    - Codebook development
    - Theme identification with supporting quotes
    - Negative case analysis (instances that do not fit the pattern)
    - Saturation assessment
    - Output: thematic structure with representative quotes in table format

3.9 Results section drafting
    - Follow the analytical sequence: data screening → descriptive → measurement model → structural model → additional analyses
    - Use APA-style reporting: statistics formatted correctly (χ²(df) = value, p = value; β = value, t = value, p = value)
    - Tables: self-contained with clear titles, notes, and abbreviations explained
    - Figures: clear, high-resolution, colorblind-friendly palette
    - NO interpretation in Results section — save for Discussion
    - Output: §4 Results section complete draft
```

**Stage 3 exit criteria**: Data screening complete, all analyses conducted and verified, results section drafted with tables and figures, all hypotheses addressed.

**References to load**: `references/quantitative-methods.md`, `references/measurement-scales.md`

### Stage 4: Discussion & Implications

**Goal**: Interpret findings, articulate theoretical contributions, derive practical implications, and acknowledge limitations.

**Input needed from user**: Results section (from Stage 3), theoretical framework and hypotheses (from Stage 2).

**Agent actions**:

```
4.1 Summary of findings
    - Restate the research purpose and key findings in plain language (no statistics)
    - Organize findings by research question or hypothesis
    - Structure: "This study examined [phenomenon] through the lens of [theory]. The results indicate that [key finding 1], [key finding 2], and [key finding 3]."
    - Output: findings summary paragraph (1-2 paragraphs)

4.2 Theoretical contributions
    - For each contribution:
        1. Recall the theoretical gap identified in the literature review
        2. State your finding that addresses this gap
        3. Explain how this finding extends, refines, or challenges existing theory
        4. Situate within the broader tourism/hospitality literature discourse
    - Distinguish between:
        - Confirming existing theory in a new context (incremental but valuable)
        - Extending theory (new mediator/moderator, boundary condition)
        - Challenging theory (unexpected finding that questions existing assumptions)
    - Use phrases like: "This finding extends [theory] by..." or "Contrary to [Author]'s proposition, our results suggest..."
    - Output: theoretical contributions section (3-5 paragraphs)

4.3 Practical / managerial implications
    - Structure by stakeholder group:
        - Destination Management Organizations (DMOs) / Tourism Boards
        - Hotel / hospitality managers / revenue managers
        - Tourism policymakers / government agencies
        - Local communities / residents
    - Each implication follows the SAR format:
        - Setting: Remind the reader of the tourism context and key finding
        - Analytical finding: State what your research found (plain language)
        - Recommendation: What this stakeholder should DO differently
    - Be specific: "Destination marketers should..." not "Marketers should..."
    - Ground recommendations in your results: "Given that X significantly influences Y (β = 0.XX, p < 0.001), destination managers should..."
    - Use actionable verbs: develop, implement, prioritize, segment, communicate, invest, redesign
    - Distinguish implications supported by findings vs. speculative recommendations
    - Output: practical implications section (3-6 paragraphs, organized by stakeholder)

4.4 Policy implications (if applicable, especially for JST, ATR)
    - Connect findings to tourism policy debates
    - Discuss: visa policy, taxation, destination carrying capacity, community benefit-sharing
    - Be evidence-based; avoid unsupported advocacy
    - Output: policy implications paragraph(s)

4.5 Limitations and future research
    - Limitations (be honest, specific, and constructive):
        - Sampling: representativeness, generalizability, single destination/context
        - Design: cross-sectional (no causality), single-source (CMB)
        - Measurement: scale adaptation issues, self-report bias
        - Context: specific time period, season, event; may not generalize
        - Method: assumptions of the chosen analytical approach
    - Future research (concrete, actionable directions):
        - For each limitation, suggest a specific future study
        - Propose: different context (other destination/hotel type), longitudinal design, multi-source data, experimental approach, different theoretical lens
        - Avoid vague directions ("future research should examine other variables")
    - Output: limitations and future research section

4.6 Discussion coherence check
    - Does every theoretical contribution trace to a specific finding?
    - Does every practical implication trace to a specific finding?
    - Are unexpected findings discussed (not ignored)?
    - Are alternative explanations for findings considered?
    - Is the discussion proportionate to the contribution size?
    - Output: revised discussion with verified coherence
```

**Stage 4 exit criteria**: Complete discussion section with findings summary, theoretical contributions, practical implications, limitations, and future research. All contributions and implications are traceable to specific results.

**Reference to load**: `references/reviewer-expectations.md`

### Stage 5: Writing & Assembly

**Goal**: Write the full paper from Introduction to Conclusion, assemble all sections, cross-check for consistency, format for target journal, and polish.

**Input needed from user**: All prior stage outputs, target journal choice.

**Agent actions**:

```
5.1 Introduction drafting
    - P1: Tourism phenomenon hook — a specific tourism/hospitality context, real example, or contemporary issue. Use a concrete destination, hotel brand, or event as the opening example.
    - P2: Generalization — broaden to the wider tourism/hospitality phenomenon; establish significance ("With international tourist arrivals reaching X billion...")
    - P3-P4: Literature gap — "Despite the importance of [phenomenon], the tourism literature has largely focused on... while neglecting..." → Explicit gap statement
    - P5: Our approach — state the theoretical lens in one sentence (no detailed literature review) and the methodological approach
    - P6: Contributions — numbered list (3-4 items): theoretical, methodological (if applicable), practical
    - P7: Paper organization — "The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on [stream 1] and [stream 2] and develops the conceptual framework and hypotheses. Section 3 describes the research methodology..."
    - Output: §1 Introduction draft

5.2 Literature review and theoretical framework
    - Organize by conceptual stream, NOT paper-by-paper chronological order:
        - Stream 1: First key construct/theory (e.g., destination image literature)
        - Stream 2: Second key construct/theory (e.g., tourist satisfaction literature)
        - Stream 3: The relationship between them (e.g., image-satisfaction-loyalty nexus)
    - For each stream:
        - Define the construct/theory
        - Review seminal and recent tourism/hospitality-specific studies
        - Identify what's been established AND what remains unknown
        - End each stream with a transition to your study
    - Hypothesis/Proposition development:
        - Each hypothesis preceded by theoretical reasoning paragraph(s)
        - Use logic: Theory X suggests A → B. In the context of tourism/hospitality, this implies... Therefore:
        - State hypothesis formally: H1: A positively influences B.
    - Include the Gap Table (from Stage 1) to visually differentiate your study
    - Include the conceptual model diagram (figure)
    - Output: §2 Literature Review and Theoretical Framework draft

5.3 Title crafting
    - Tourism paper title conventions:
        - Informative titles: "The Role of X in Shaping Y: Evidence from Z"
        - Two-part titles: "X and Y: The Mediating Role of Z in Tourism Context"
        - Key elements: main constructs, theoretical lens, context (destination/hotel type), relationship
    - Avoid: overly clever/punny titles, question titles (except for ATR occasionally)
    - Include: key search terms for discoverability
    - Length: typically 12-20 words
    - Output: 3-5 candidate titles

5.4 Abstract writing
    - Write LAST, after the full paper is drafted
    - Structure (Farquhar 5-sentence formula adapted for tourism):
        1. Background/phenomenon: What tourism/hospitality phenomenon and why important? (1 sentence)
        2. Gap/purpose: What gap does this study fill? (1 sentence)
        3. Method: How was the study conducted? Include context, sample, and analytical approach. (1 sentence)
        4. Key findings: What are the most important results? (2-3 sentences)
        5. Implications: What are the theoretical and practical contributions? (1-2 sentences)
    - Keywords: 5-7 keywords (after abstract); use standard tourism terminology
    - Word limits: TM 150-250, ATR 150-250, JTR 150-200, IJHM 150-200, IJCHM 150-200, JST 150-250
    - Output: Abstract + keywords

5.5 Conclusion drafting
    - Summary: 2-3 sentences restating the phenomenon, theoretical lens, method, and key finding
    - Significance: 1-2 sentences on why this matters for tourism scholarship and practice
    - Closing: 1 sentence looking forward
    - Avoid: repeating the abstract verbatim, introducing new information, overclaiming
    - Output: §6 Conclusion draft

5.6 Cross-section consistency check
    - Introduction claims ↔ Discussion section coverage (1:1 mapping of contributions to discussion)
    - Hypotheses stated in §2 ↔ results reported in §4 ↔ discussed in §5 (every hypothesis accounted for)
    - Constructs in conceptual model ↔ measures in methodology ↔ variables in results
    - All tables and figures referenced in text, in numerical order
    - All citations appear in References; all References cited in text
    - Abstract claims substantiated in body; no claims in abstract not supported by results
    - Terminology consistent throughout (don't use "visitor" in one section and "tourist" in another without reason)
    - Output: consistency check report with corrections

5.7 Formatting for target journal
    - Apply journal-specific formatting:
        - TM: APA 7th ed.; double-spaced; separate title page; tables/figures at end or embedded
        - ATR: APA 7th ed.; double-spaced; title page with acknowledgments
        - JTR: APA 7th ed.; double-spaced; structured abstract
        - IJHM: APA 7th ed.
        - IJCHM: APA 7th ed.
        - JST: APA 7th ed.
    - Verify page budget compliance (see Page Budgets table below)
    - Format tables with APA style (horizontal lines only, clear headings, notes)
    - Format figures: high resolution (300+ dpi), clear labels, colorblind-friendly
    - Output: formatted manuscript

5.8 Reference verification
    - Verify all citations against DOI/programmatic fetch
    - Check: no hallucinated references (author-year mismatch, wrong journal, invented paper)
    - Format references per APA 7th ed.
    - Verify that all cited tourism/hospitality scales are correctly attributed to their originators
    - Output: verified reference list

5.9 Final polish
    - Run language polish (use ppw:polish or equivalent)
    - Check for tourism/hospitality terminology accuracy
    - Check for consistent tense (past tense for literature review and results; present tense for discussion and implications)
    - Check for wordiness; tighten prose
    - Output: polished final draft
```

**Stage 5 exit criteria**: Complete first draft with all sections, consistency verified, journal formatting applied, references verified, language polished.

**References to load**: `references/writing-patterns.md`, `references/reviewer-expectations.md`, `references/journal-characteristics.md`

## Writing Patterns

### Pattern 1: The Motivating Tourism Example

Open the introduction with a concrete, specific example from the tourism/hospitality world. Name a real destination, hotel group, or event. Use real numbers when available.

**Template**:
> In 2023, [Destination X] welcomed [N] million international visitors, generating [\$Y] billion in tourism receipts. Yet, despite this growth, hotel occupancy rates fell to [Z%], suggesting a disconnect between arrivals and actual tourism yield. This paradox — growing visitor numbers alongside declining profitability — raises a fundamental question about [phenomenon your paper addresses].

**Required by**: TM, JTR (strongly recommended), IJHM. **Valued by**: ATR, JST, IJCHM.

### Pattern 2: The Theoretical Lens Positioning

Tourism papers must explicitly position themselves within a theoretical tradition. State your theoretical lens early (in the introduction) and justify why this lens is appropriate for the tourism/hospitality context.

**Template**:
> This study draws on [Theory] ([Key Author(s)], [Year]) as its theoretical foundation. [Theory] is particularly suited to the tourism context because [reason 1: unique characteristics of tourism as a product/service] and [reason 2: nature of tourist behavior/experience]. While [Theory] has been extensively applied in [parent discipline], its application in tourism/hospitality research remains limited to [specific subset of studies], and its explanatory power for [your phenomenon] has not been examined.

**Required by**: ATR (critical). **Valued by**: TM, JTR, JST.

### Pattern 3: The Gap Table

A structured comparison table positioning your study against 10-15 key references.

**Template columns**: Author(s), Context (destination/hotel type), Theory/Framework, Method, Sample, Key Constructs, Key Findings.

Your paper occupies the last row, with explicit differentiation (bold or italic) showing what you add.

**Required by**: TM, ATR (strongly recommended for quantitative papers). **Valued by**: JTR, JST, IJHM.

### Pattern 4: Hypothesis Development with Theoretical Reasoning

Each hypothesis must be preceded by a theoretical reasoning paragraph. Never present a bare hypothesis.

**Template**:
> [Theory] posits that [mechanism]. In the context of tourism, [application to tourism/hospitality]. Empirical evidence supports this relationship. For instance, [Author] (Year) found that [finding] in [context]. However, whether this relationship holds in [your specific tourism context] remains unexamined. Therefore:
>
> **H1**: [Construct A] positively influences [Construct B].

### Pattern 5: The SAR Practical Implication

Each practical implication should be a self-contained mini-section.

**Template**:
> **Practical Implication 1: [Action-oriented heading]**
>
> **Setting**: [Brief reminder of the tourism context — destination, hotel, or policy setting]
>
> **Finding**: Our results show that [plain-language statement of key finding]. Specifically, [key statistic in accessible terms, e.g., "a one-unit increase in destination image was associated with a 0.XX unit increase in tourist satisfaction"].
>
> **Recommendation**: [Stakeholder group] should [specific, actionable action]. For example, [concrete illustration in a tourism/hospitality context].

**Required by**: TM. **Strongly valued by**: IJHM, JTR, IJCHM.

### Pattern 6: Triangulation in Mixed-Methods Tourism Research

When using mixed methods, explicitly describe how qualitative and quantitative findings converge, complement, or diverge.

**Template**:
> The quantitative results indicate that [finding], while the qualitative findings reveal that [finding]. These findings [converge on / provide complementary perspectives on / diverge in their implications for] [phenomenon]. The convergence suggests [interpretation]. [If divergent:] The divergence may reflect [explanation], highlighting the value of the mixed-methods design in capturing the complexity of [tourism/hospitality phenomenon].

**Valued by**: ATR, JST (especially community-based tourism research).

### Pattern 7: Contextualizing Limitations

Tourism papers should avoid generic limitations and instead contextualize them within the specific tourism/hospitality setting.

**Template**:
> Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, data were collected at a single [destination type / hotel category / event] during [season], which may limit generalizability to [other destination types / off-peak periods / different event types]. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding [relationship], although the proposed direction of effects is grounded in [theory]. Future research could employ a longitudinal design tracking [tourists/guests] across multiple [visits/stays]. Third, [additional contextualized limitation].

## Quick Checklist Before Submission

- [ ] Target journal fits the paper's phenomenon-method-contribution profile (verify via journal selection guide)
- [ ] Introduction: tourism phenomenon hook (motivating example), explicit gap statement, theoretical lens named, numbered contributions, paper organization paragraph
- [ ] Literature review: organized by conceptual stream (not paper-by-paper), gap table present, hypotheses/propositions grounded in theory with reasoning paragraphs
- [ ] Methodology: research paradigm stated, sampling strategy justified, all measures sourced from established tourism/hospitality scales (with citations), data collection procedure described, analytical approach justified, common method bias remedies described
- [ ] Measurement model (if SEM/CFA): model fit indices (χ²/df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR), factor loadings (> 0.70), CR (> 0.70), AVE (> 0.50), discriminant validity (Fornell-Larcker and/or HTMT)
- [ ] Results: descriptive statistics, hypothesis tests with effect sizes, mediation tested with bootstrapped CIs, robustness checks present
- [ ] Common method bias: assessed (Harman's single-factor and/or marker variable), discussed as limitation if single-source
- [ ] Discussion: findings summarized in plain language, theoretical contributions explicitly linked to theory from §2, practical implications organized by stakeholder and following SAR format, limitations contextualized to tourism/hospitality setting, future research directions are specific and actionable
- [ ] Theoretical contribution: clearly articulated — what does this paper ADD to tourism/hospitality theory that was not known before?
- [ ] Practical contribution: specific, contextualized, actionable — not "managers should improve service quality"
- [ ] Conclusion: concise synthesis, no new information, no abstract repetition
- [ ] Abstract: written last, contains all 5 elements (phenomenon, gap, method, findings, implications), within word limit
- [ ] Cross-section consistency: all hypotheses from §2 tested in §4 and discussed in §5, all constructs defined in §2 measured in §3 and analyzed in §4, all tables/figures referenced, all citations verified
- [ ] Page/word budget: within journal limits (all-inclusive count)
- [ ] Formatting: APA 7th ed., journal-specific requirements met
- [ ] All references verified via DOI/programmatic fetch (no hallucinated references)
- [ ] Language: consistent tense (past for lit review/results, present for discussion), tourism/hospitality terminology accurate, jargon-free where possible
- [ ] Cover letter: tailored to journal, highlights contribution, suggests potential reviewers
