# Measurement Scales in Tourism Research

This reference catalogues the validated scales most commonly used in top-tier tourism journals, details the scale development and validation process, addresses common method bias, and specifies reporting standards for measurement models.

---

## 1. Validated Scales Commonly Used in Tourism

### 1.1 Destination Image

**Echtner and Ritchie (1993, 2003) — Destination Image Framework**
The most cited framework for measuring destination image. It conceptualizes destination image along three continua: attribute-holistic, functional-psychological, and common-unique.

**Recommended Measurement Approach**:
1. **Structured (attribute-based) component**: Likert-scale items measuring specific destination attributes. Typical dimensions include:
   - Natural attractions (scenery, climate, beaches)
   - Cultural attractions (museums, festivals, heritage sites)
   - Infrastructure (accommodation, transport, restaurants)
   - Atmosphere (safe, friendly, exotic, relaxing)
   - Value (reasonable prices, value for money)

2. **Unstructured (holistic) component**: Open-ended questions to capture holistic impressions and unique features.
   - "What images or characteristics come to mind when you think of [destination] as a travel destination?"
   - "How would you describe the atmosphere or mood you would expect to find at [destination]?"
   - "Please list any distinctive or unique tourist attractions you associate with [destination]."

**Reporting**: Report both structured and unstructured findings. The unstructured component provides qualitative context for the quantitative scores. Quote distinctive participant responses to demonstrate the unique dimension.

**Alternative Destination Image Scales**:
- **Beerli and Martin (2004)**: Comprehensive scale with five dimensions (natural resources, general infrastructure, tourist infrastructure, tourist leisure and recreation, culture-history-art, political and economic factors). 24 items.
- **Byon and Zhang (2010)**: Scale for destination image in sport tourism events. Four dimensions: event image, infrastructure, natural environment, social environment.
- **Stylidis, Shani, and Belhassen (2017)**: Three-dimensional model: cognitive image, affective image, conative image. 15 items. Report the three-factor CFA model with fit indices.

### 1.2 Place Attachment

**Williams and Vaske (2003) — Place Attachment Scale**
The dominant scale for measuring tourists' emotional bonds to destinations.

**Two Dimensions**:
1. **Place Identity** (6 items): The destination as part of the self-concept.
   - "I identify strongly with this place."
   - "This place means a lot to me."
   - "I am very attached to this place."
   - "Visiting this place says a lot about who I am."
   - "This place feels like part of me."
   - "I feel a sense of belonging to this place."

2. **Place Dependence** (6 items): The destination's ability to satisfy activity needs relative to alternatives.
   - "[This place] is the best place for what I like to do."
   - "No other place can compare to [this place]."
   - "I get more satisfaction out of visiting [this place] than any other."
   - "Doing what I do at [this place] is more important to me than doing it elsewhere."
   - "I wouldn't substitute any other place for doing the types of things I do at [this place]."
   - "[This place] is my favorite place for recreational activities."

**Factor Structure**: Two-factor correlated model. CFA must report fit indices. The two-factor model should be compared against a one-factor model to demonstrate discriminant validity using a chi-square difference test.

**Adaptation for Tourism**: The original scale was developed for recreational settings (parks, trails). For tourism destinations, consider the adaptation by Ramkissoon, Smith, and Weiler (2013), which incorporates four dimensions: place dependence, place identity, place affect, and place social bonding.

**Reporting**: "Confirmatory factor analysis supported the two-factor correlated model: chi-square(53) = 142.30, p < 0.01; CFI = 0.97; TLI = 0.96; RMSEA = 0.052 (90% CI: 0.041-0.063); SRMR = 0.036. The two-factor model fit significantly better than the one-factor model (delta chi-square(1) = 215.46, p < 0.01), supporting discriminant validity. CR for place identity = 0.91; CR for place dependence = 0.88."

### 1.3 Memorable Tourism Experience (MTE)

**Kim, Ritchie, and McCormick (2012) — Memorable Tourism Experience Scale**
One of the most cited tourism scales in the past decade. Measures the dimensions of experiences that tourists remember and recall later.

**Seven Dimensions (24 items)**:
1. **Hedonism** (3 items): Thrilled, excited, enjoyed
2. **Refreshment** (3 items): Liberating, refreshed, rejuvenated
3. **Local culture** (3 items): Local culture, local people, local customs
4. **Meaningfulness** (3 items): Meaningful, did something important, learned about myself
5. **Involvement** (3 items): Visited a place I really wanted to go, enjoyed activities I really wanted to do, interested in main activities
6. **Knowledge** (3 items): Exploratory, knowledge, new culture
7. **Novelty** (3 items): Once-in-a-lifetime, unique, different from previous experiences

**Additional items**: Kim et al. included items on perceived value, satisfaction, and behavioral intentions used as outcome variables, not dimensions of MTE itself.

**Factor Structure**: Seven-factor correlated model (second-order model is also common). Kim et al. (2012) reported acceptable fit: chi-square/df = 2.56, CFI = 0.94, RMSEA = 0.06.

**Dimensions frequently collapsed in tourism applications**:
- Hedonism and refreshment are sometimes combined into "hedonic experience"
- The full 24-item scale is long; some tourism studies use a 16-item short form
- **If you shorten the scale**: Pre-test the shortened version with CFA. Do not simply select items with the highest loadings from the original, as this inflates fit statistics in your sample. Report why items were dropped and test the short form on an independent hold-out sample.

**Reporting**: Cite the original Kim et al. (2012) JTR paper. Report CFA results if you adapt the scale (new language, new population, new context). Include factor loadings, AVE, and CR for each dimension.

### 1.4 Service Quality (SERVQUAL Adapted for Tourism)

**Parasuraman, Zeithaml, and Berry (1988) — SERVQUAL**
The original SERVQUAL measures service quality as the gap between customer expectations (E) and perceived performance (P): SQ = P minus E.

**Five Original Dimensions (22 items)**:
1. Tangibles: Physical facilities, equipment, appearance of personnel
2. Reliability: Ability to perform the promised service dependably and accurately
3. Responsiveness: Willingness to help customers and provide prompt service
4. Assurance: Knowledge and courtesy of employees and ability to inspire trust
5. Empathy: Caring, individualized attention the firm provides its customers

**Adaptations for Tourism Contexts**:
- **HOLSERV** (Mei, Dean, and White 1999): SERVQUAL adapted for hotels. Three dimensions: employees, tangibles, reliability. 14 items.
- **LODGSERV** (Knutson et al. 1990): SERVQUAL adapted for lodging. 26 items.
- **DINESERV** (Stevens, Knutson, and Patton 1995): SERVQUAL adapted for restaurants. 29 items.
- **HISTOQUAL** (Frochot and Hughes 2000): SERVQUAL adapted for historic houses/heritage sites. 24 items.
- **ECOSERV** (Khan 2003): SERVQUAL adapted for ecotourism. 30 items.
- **TOURQUAL** (Mondo and Farias, multiple versions): Comprehensive scale for tourism service quality at the destination level.

**Critical Issue — Expectations vs. Perceptions-Only**:
The original SERVQUAL measures expectations AND perceptions (gap scores). Cronin and Taylor (1992) argued that performance-only measurement (SERVPERF) has better predictive validity. In tourism:
- For **cross-sectional** satisfaction research, use SERVPERF (perceptions only) — higher predictive validity for overall satisfaction.
- For **diagnostic** service quality assessment (identifying where to improve), use SERVQUAL (gap scores) — identifies specific service failures.
- State your choice and justify it: "We used the performance-only approach because our focus is on predicting overall satisfaction rather than diagnosing specific service gaps."

**Reporting**: "Following the SERVPERF approach (Cronin & Taylor, 1992), we measured service quality using 22 items adapted from SERVQUAL (Parasuraman et al., 1988), reworded for the hotel context. Items were measured on a 7-point Likert scale. CFA results supported a five-factor structure: chi-square(199) = 512.34, CFI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.06."

### 1.5 Tourist Satisfaction

No single dominant scale — multiple validated measures exist.

- **Oliver's Expectation-Disconfirmation Model**: Satisfaction as a function of the disconfirmation between expectations and performance. Measured with single-item overall satisfaction plus multi-item cognitive and affective satisfaction.
- **Spreng, MacKenzie, and Olshavsky (1996)**: Distinguishes attribute satisfaction (satisfaction with specific attributes) from overall satisfaction. Both should be measured.
- **Gallarza, Arteaga, and Gil-Saura (2017)**: Multi-dimensional tourist satisfaction including cognitive, affective, and conative dimensions.

**Recommended Measurement**:
1. **Overall satisfaction** (3 items, 7-point Likert):
   - "Overall, I am satisfied with my visit to [destination]."
   - "My experience at [destination] exceeded my expectations."
   - "This visit was a good decision."

2. **Attribute satisfaction** (multi-item, adapted to context):
   - Accommodation, food, attractions, transport, hospitality, value for money, safety, cleanliness, etc.

**Reporting**: Avoid measuring satisfaction with a single item unless justified. Multi-item measures capture cognitive and affective components. Report alpha and CR for the satisfaction scale. Report the correlation between attribute satisfaction and overall satisfaction (typically 0.50-0.70 in tourism).

### 1.6 Behavioral Intention / Destination Loyalty

**Composite Loyalty Scale** (adapted from marketing to tourism; recommended by Bigne, Sanchez, and Sanchez 2001; Chen and Tsai 2007):

**Three Dimensions**:

1. **Revisit intention** (3 items):
   - "I intend to visit [destination] again in the future."
   - "I plan to revisit [destination] within the next [timeframe]."
   - "I would make an effort to return to [destination]."

2. **Word-of-mouth intention** (3 items):
   - "I would recommend [destination] to friends and family."
   - "I would say positive things about [destination] to others."
   - "I would encourage others to visit [destination]."

3. **Willingness to pay more / tolerance** (3 items):
   - "I would be willing to pay a higher price to visit [destination]."
   - "I would still visit [destination] even if prices increased."
   - "I consider [destination] my first choice among similar destinations."

**Composite Index vs. Multi-Dimensional**: Tourism studies vary in whether they report loyalty as a single composite or three sub-dimensions. If theoretically distinct (e.g., revisit intention and WOM have different predictors), report sub-dimensions. If parsimony is preferred, report the composite with justification and CFA evidence that a second-order factor structure fits.

**Note on Attitudinal vs. Behavioral Loyalty**: The scales above measure ATTITUDINAL loyalty (intention). Actual BEHAVIORAL loyalty (repeat visits, actual WOM) rarely matches stated intentions. If possible, measure actual behavior: "How many times have you visited [destination] in the past 5 years?" Correlate stated intention with past behavior; the correlation is typically 0.30-0.50 in tourism (the intention-behavior gap).

### 1.7 Perceived Value

**PERVAL (Sweeney and Soutar 2001)** adapted for tourism:

Four dimensions (19 items original; commonly shortened to 12 items for tourism):
1. **Functional value (quality/performance)**: "This hotel has consistent quality."
2. **Functional value (price/value for money)**: "This hotel is reasonably priced."
3. **Emotional value**: "Visiting this destination makes me feel good/happy/relaxed."
4. **Social value**: "Staying at this hotel makes a good impression on others."

**Sanchez et al. (2006)** developed a tourism-specific perceived value scale (GLOVAL) with six dimensions: functional value (establishment), functional value (personnel), functional value (service quality), functional value (price), emotional value, social value. 24 items total.

**Reporting**: "Perceived value was measured using 12 items adapted from Sweeney and Soutar (2001) and Sanchez et al. (2006). The four-factor CFA model demonstrated acceptable fit: chi-square(48) = 128.90, CFI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.057. AVE ranged from 0.54 (social value) to 0.72 (emotional value)."

### 1.8 Destination Competitiveness

**Ritchie and Crouch (2003, 2011) — Destination Competitiveness and Sustainability Model**

This is a conceptual framework rather than a measurement scale, but it is the standard reference for destination competitiveness research. Key dimensions:
1. **Core resources and attractors**: Physiography, culture, market ties, activities, events, superstructure
2. **Supporting factors and resources**: Infrastructure, accessibility, facilitating resources, hospitality, enterprise, political will
3. **Destination management**: Organization, marketing, quality of service, information, monitoring
4. **Destination policy, planning, and development**: Philosophy, vision, positioning, competitive analysis
5. **Qualifying and amplifying determinants**: Location, safety, cost, interdependencies, carrying capacity
6. **Global (macro) environment**: Economic, social, technological, political, environmental

**Dwyer and Kim (2003)** developed indicators for each dimension. Subsequent researchers have operationalized subsets of these indicators in surveys of destination managers and tourists.

**Measurement Challenge**: Destination competitiveness is inherently multi-level (firm, destination, nation) and multi-stakeholder (tourists, residents, industry, government). A single survey cannot capture all dimensions. Tourism papers typically focus on a subset of indicators from one stakeholder perspective.

**Gooroochurn and Sugiyarto (2005)** proposed the Tourism Competitiveness Index with eight dimensions measured by secondary data (price, openness, technology, infrastructure, human tourism, social development, environment, human resources).

## 2. Scale Development Process

When existing scales are inadequate or inappropriate for your tourism context, follow the systematic scale development process (DeVellis 2017; Churchill 1979; Hinkin 1998).

### 2.1 Step 1: Construct Definition and Domain Specification

**Define the construct** with conceptual clarity BEFORE generating items. Tourism papers often rush to item generation without adequate construct definition, leading to construct proliferation (the "Jingle-Jangle Fallacy" — using different names for the same construct, or the same name for different constructs).

**A proper construct definition includes**:
1. The domain of the construct (what it covers)
2. The boundaries of the construct (what it does NOT cover)
3. The dimensionality (unidimensional vs. multidimensional)
4. The distinction from related constructs (discriminant validity at the conceptual level)
5. The theoretical relationship with antecedent and consequent constructs (nomological network)

**Example (construct definition for "Sustainable Travel Behavior")**:
> Sustainable travel behavior is defined as the extent to which tourists make choices that minimize negative environmental, social, and economic impacts of their travel. It is conceptualized as a multi-dimensional construct comprising environmental behaviors (e.g., resource conservation, waste reduction), social behaviors (e.g., respecting local culture, supporting local businesses), and economic behaviors (e.g., choosing locally-owned accommodations). It is distinct from general pro-environmental behavior (which is not travel-specific) and from environmental attitudes (which are evaluative, not behavioral).

### 2.2 Step 2: Item Generation

**Sources of items**:
1. **Deductive** (from theory): Items generated from the theoretical definition and its dimensions. Cover the full conceptual breadth of each dimension.
2. **Inductive** (from qualitative data): Items generated from interviews, focus groups, or open-ended survey responses. Tourism scale development frequently uses Phase 1 qualitative research as the basis for Phase 2 item generation.
3. **Existing scales**: Adapted from validated scales in related domains, with items reworded for the tourism context.

**Initial item pool size**: Generate 2-3 times the number of items you intend to retain. For a 15-item scale (intended final), generate 30-45 initial items.

**Item writing principles**:
- Simple, clear language (avoid academic jargon — the scale may be administered to tourists, not tourism professors)
- One idea per item (avoid double-barreled items: "The hotel was clean and comfortable")
- Avoid leading or socially desirable items ("Responsible tourists always recycle at their hotel" — who would disagree?)
- Match item wording to the response scale (frequency items with frequency scales; agreement items with Likert scales)
- Include reverse-coded items ONLY if theoretically justified and tested for comprehension (reverse-coded items in tourism surveys often create method artifacts, especially with non-native English speakers)

### 2.3 Step 3: Content Validity Assessment

**Expert Review** (3-7 experts):
1. Provide experts with the construct definition and the item pool
2. Experts rate each item on: (a) relevance to the construct (1 = not relevant, 3 = highly relevant), (b) clarity of wording, (c) whether the item captures a unique aspect (not redundant)
3. Compute the **Content Validity Index (CVI)**:
   - Item-level CVI (I-CVI): Proportion of experts rating the item as "quite relevant" or "highly relevant" (2 or 3). I-CVI >= 0.78 for 6-10 experts (Lynn 1986).
   - Scale-level CVI (S-CVI/Ave): Average I-CVI across all items. S-CVI/Ave >= 0.90 (Polit and Beck 2006).
4. Revise or delete items with low I-CVI

**Pilot Testing**:
- Administer the refined item pool to 50-100 respondents from the target population
- Conduct cognitive interviews with 5-10 respondents: "What did you think this item was asking?" (to check comprehension)
- Compute item statistics: means, standard deviations, item-total correlations
- Delete items with ceiling/floor effects, low variability, or low item-total correlations (< 0.30)

### 2.4 Step 4: Scale Purification (First Survey, N >= 300)

**Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)**:
1. Check factorability: KMO > 0.60 (ideally > 0.80) and Bartlett's test of sphericity significant (p < 0.05)
2. Extraction method: Principal Axis Factoring (PAF) if data are non-normal; Maximum Likelihood (ML) if multivariate normal. **Avoid PCA for scale development** — it models total variance, inflating loadings. State your extraction method and justify it.
3. Rotation: Oblique (Promax or Direct Oblimin) if factors are expected to correlate (they typically are in tourism). Orthogonal rotation (Varimax) ONLY if factors are theoretically independent — justify this.
4. Factor retention:
   - Eigenvalues > 1 (Kaiser criterion) — liberal, use with caution
   - Scree plot (Cattell 1966) — visual inspection; report
   - **Parallel analysis** (Horn 1965) — preferred; retains factors with eigenvalues exceeding those from random data. Report the number of factors retained by parallel analysis.
   - Minimum average partial (MAP)
5. Item retention: Loadings > 0.50 on the primary factor and cross-loadings < 0.30 on other factors. Items with cross-loadings in the 0.30-0.40 range may be retained with theoretical justification.
6. Communalities > 0.40

**Reliability Assessment**:
- Cronbach's alpha > 0.70 (Nunnally 1978). Report for each dimension.
- Corrected item-total correlations > 0.30
- "Alpha if item deleted" — if deleting one item substantially increases alpha (by > 0.03), consider removing it (but only if also theoretically justified)

### 2.5 Step 5: Scale Validation (Second Survey, N >= 300, independent sample)

**Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA)**:

1. **Model specification**: Specify the measurement model based on the EFA results. Each item loads on ONE latent factor. Factor variances are fixed to 1 (or one loading per factor is fixed to 1). Error covariances are fixed to 0 unless modification indices AND theory suggest correlated errors.

2. **Model fit assessment** (report at minimum):
   - chi-square, df, chi-square/df (< 2.0 good, < 3.0 acceptable)
   - CFI >= 0.95
   - TLI >= 0.95
   - RMSEA < 0.06 (with 90% CI)
   - SRMR < 0.08
   - Report modification indices > 10.0 only if they suggest substantive (not just statistical) model improvements.

3. **Convergent validity**:
   - Standardized factor loadings > 0.50 (ideally > 0.70) and significant (p < 0.05)
   - AVE (Average Variance Extracted) > 0.50 for each construct
   - CR (Composite Reliability) > 0.70 for each construct

4. **Discriminant validity**:
   - **HTMT (Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio of Correlations)** is the preferred method (Henseler, Ringle, and Sarstedt 2015). HTMT < 0.85 (strict) or < 0.90 (liberal for conceptually similar constructs). Report HTMT and its bootstrap 95% CI (upper bound < 1.0). This replaces the Fornell-Larcker criterion, which has been shown to lack sensitivity.
   - **Fornell-Larcker criterion** (acceptable for CB-SEM but less sensitive): AVE for each construct > squared correlation between that construct and any other.
   - **Cross-loadings**: Each item's loading on its intended construct should exceed its loading on any other construct. (Less diagnostic than HTMT.)

5. **Alternative model comparison**: Test your hypothesized factor structure against plausible alternatives using delta chi-square tests:
   - One-factor model (all items load on a single construct — tests against common method bias and construct distinctiveness)
   - Uncorrelated factors model (if factors should be distinguishable)
   - Second-order model (if a higher-order construct is theoretically justified)
   - Report the significant delta chi-square showing your hypothesized model fits better.

### 2.6 Step 6: Nomological Validity

Test whether the construct relates to other constructs as theoretically predicted:
- Does it correlate positively with constructs it should relate to?
- Does it correlate weakly or not at all with constructs it should be distinct from?
- Does it predict outcomes it should predict?
- Report the full structural model: chi-square, df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR, plus path coefficients and their significance.

## 3. Common Method Bias (CMB)

Tourism research relies heavily on self-report surveys — a classic recipe for CMB. When a single survey captures both predictor and criterion variables, shared method variance can inflate (or deflate) observed relationships.

### 3.1 Procedural Remedies (Ex Ante)

These are STRONGER than statistical remedies because they prevent CMB rather than diagnosing it. Apply as many as feasible:

1. **Temporal separation**: Collect predictor and criterion measures at different times. In tourism, survey tourists during their trip (predictors: destination image, motivation) and follow up one week after return (criteria: satisfaction, behavioral intention). Report the time gap and response rate for the follow-up.

2. **Psychological separation**: Use different cover stories or separate sections of the questionnaire. Separate predictor and criterion items with filler items or demographic questions.

3. **Methodological separation**: Use different response formats (e.g., semantic differential scales for predictors, Likert scales for criteria). This reduces the tendency for respondents to maintain a consistent response pattern.

4. **Protect anonymity and reduce evaluation apprehension**: Assure respondents there are no right or wrong answers. This is especially important in tourism when surveying employees about their organization or tourists about sensitive topics.

5. **Counterbalance question order**: Randomize the order of construct blocks. If this is impractical (e.g., logical flow requires image before satisfaction), acknowledge this limitation.

6. **Improve scale items**: Avoid ambiguous items, double-barreled items, and complex syntax. Use clear, specific language. Pre-test items with cognitive interviews.

### 3.2 Statistical Remedies (Ex Post)

These are diagnostics, not remedies. They detect CMB but cannot remove it.

**1. Harman's Single-Factor Test**:
- Conduct an unrotated EFA with all items from all constructs
- If a single factor emerges, or if one factor accounts for > 50% of total variance, CMB is a concern
- **Critical caveat**: This is widely criticized as insensitive (Fuller et al. 2016). A non-significant Harman test does NOT prove absence of CMB. Yet many tourism reviewers still expect it.
- Reporting: "Harman's single-factor test revealed that the first unrotated factor accounted for 32.4% of the total variance (below the 50% threshold), suggesting that CMB is not a major concern. However, given the limitations of this test (Podsakoff et al. 2003), we also conducted a CFA marker variable analysis."

**2. Common Latent Factor (CLF) in CFA**:
- Add an unmeasured latent method factor to the CFA model. All items load on their substantive construct AND on the CLF.
- Compare the standardized regression weights with and without the CLF. If differences are < 0.20, CMB is not substantial.
- Report both sets of loadings and the differences.

**3. Marker Variable Technique** (Lindell and Whitney 2001):
- Include a marker variable — a construct theoretically unrelated to at least one substantive construct.
- The smallest positive correlation among substantive constructs serves as a proxy for method variance.
- Adjust all substantive correlations by partialling out the marker correlation.
- Report original and adjusted correlations. If the significance pattern does not change, CMB is not driving results.
- **Choosing a marker in tourism research**: Include a construct like "attitude toward the survey format" or "social desirability" (measured with a short-form Marlowe-Crowne scale).

**4. CFA Marker Technique** (Williams et al. 2010):
- More sophisticated than the Lindell-Whitney approach. Uses a marker latent variable (not just a correlation proxy).
- Compares four nested models: Baseline, Method-C, Method-U, Method-R. The pattern of fit improvement identifies the nature and magnitude of method variance.
- This is the most rigorous approach but rarely used in tourism due to its complexity. If you use it, provide a clear explanation for reviewers.

**Reporting**: "Several procedural and statistical remedies were employed to address CMB. Procedurally, we (a) assured respondent anonymity, (b) counterbalanced construct order, and (c) used different response formats for predictor and criterion variables. Statistically, Harman's single-factor test indicated that the first factor accounted for 28.7% of variance (below 50%). Additionally, the CFA with a common latent factor showed that standardized loadings changed by an average of 0.06 (range: 0.01-0.12), well below the 0.20 threshold, suggesting that CMB is not a substantial threat to our findings."

## 4. Reporting Measurement Models

### 4.1 What Must Be Reported

For every CFA measurement model in your tourism paper:

| Element | Required Detail |
|---------|----------------|
| **Model specification** | Number of factors, items per factor, whether factors correlated or orthogonal |
| **Fit indices** | chi-square, df, chi-square/df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA (with 90% CI), SRMR |
| **Factor loadings** | Standardized loadings for all items (table), significance (t-values or bootstrap CIs) |
| **Convergent validity** | AVE > 0.50 per construct, CR > 0.70 per construct |
| **Discriminant validity** | HTMT < 0.85 (preferred), or Fornell-Larcker (if CB-SEM) |
| **Reliability** | Cronbach's alpha AND composite reliability for each construct |
| **Alternative models** | One-factor model fit (for comparison), delta chi-square test |
| **Software** | Package and version (e.g., AMOS 26, Mplus 8.4, lavaan 0.6-12) |
| **Estimation method** | ML, MLR (robust), WLSMV (for categorical indicators), etc. |
| **Missing data** | How handled (listwise, FIML, multiple imputation) and proportion missing |

### 4.2 CFA Reporting Template

```
A confirmatory factor analysis was conducted using Mplus 8.4 with
maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors (MLR).
The six-factor measurement model (24 items) demonstrated acceptable
fit to the data: chi-square(237) = 512.34, p < 0.01; chi-square/df =
2.16; CFI = 0.96; TLI = 0.95; RMSEA = 0.048 (90% CI: 0.042-0.054);
SRMR = 0.042. All standardized factor loadings were significant
(p < 0.001) and ranged from 0.68 to 0.91 (see Table 2). Composite
reliability ranged from 0.82 to 0.94, exceeding the 0.70 threshold

Discriminant validity was assessed using the HTMT criterion
(Henseler et al., 2015). All HTMT values were below 0.85 (range:
0.32-0.78), and the bootstrap 95% confidence intervals did not
include 1.0, confirming discriminant validity. The hypothesized
six-factor model fit significantly better than a one-factor model
(delta chi-square(15) = 1847.32, p < 0.001), providing further

(respondent anonymity, item randomization) and statistical

```

## 5. Common Scale-Related Mistakes

1. **Using unvalidated ad-hoc scales**: Assembling items from multiple sources without CFA validation. If you create a "new" scale by combining items, it IS a new scale and requires validation.

2. **Ignoring measurement invariance**: If comparing groups (male vs. female tourists, different nationalities), test measurement invariance (configural, metric, scalar) before comparing latent means. Report delta CFI (< 0.01) and delta RMSEA (< 0.015) for invariance testing.

3. **Single-item measures for complex constructs**: Measuring satisfaction, image, or loyalty with a single item. Justify single-item use or use multi-item scales.

4. **Neglecting translation procedures**: Surveying tourists in their native language without proper translation-back-translation. Use: forward translation by bilingual expert, back-translation by independent bilingual expert, reconciliation of discrepancies, and cognitive pre-testing in the target language.

5. **Alpha inflation from redundant items**: Including nearly identical items ("I enjoyed my visit," "I liked my visit," "My visit was enjoyable") inflates alpha without improving validity. Items should capture different facets of the construct.

6. **HTMT vs. Fornell-Larcker confusion**: Many tourism papers use Fornell-Larcker when PLS-SEM reviewers expect HTMT. If using PLS-SEM, use HTMT as your primary discriminant validity test; Fornell-Larcker is supplementary.

7. **Ignoring item-level diagnostics**: Reporting only construct-level statistics (alpha, AVE) without examining individual items. Low-loading items (< 0.50) with high cross-loadings should be flagged and potentially removed, even if alpha remains acceptable.
