# Reviewer Expectations for Top Tourism Journals

This reference covers what reviewers look for at each major tourism journal, common criticisms in review reports, and how to preempt and address them. Understanding reviewer psychology and journal-specific expectations is essential for successful publication.

---

## 1. Universal Expectations (All Tourism Journals)

Every reviewer at every top tourism journal evaluates papers on these dimensions:

| Dimension | Weight | Key Question |
|-----------|--------|-------------|
| **Theoretical Contribution** | 30% | Does the paper advance tourism theory? Is the contribution clear and non-trivial? |
| **Methodological Rigor** | 25% | Are the methods appropriate, correctly applied, and thoroughly reported? |
| **Relevance to Tourism** | 20% | Is this a tourism paper, or could it be published in a generic management/marketing journal? |
| **Clarity and Structure** | 15% | Is the paper well-organized, clearly written, and easy to follow? |
| **Positioning** | 10% | Is the paper properly situated in the tourism literature (not just generic social science)? |

## 2. Journal-Specific Reviewer Priorities

### 2.1 Tourism Management (TM)

**Publisher**: Elsevier
**Impact Factor**: ~12.0 (2024)
**Acceptance Rate**: ~8-12%
**Typical Review Rounds**: 2-3

**What Reviewers REALLY Look For**:

1. **Theoretical contribution is paramount**: TM reviewers expect a clear, well-articulated theoretical contribution. Papers that are purely descriptive, applied, or that simply "apply theory X to context Y" without advancing theory will be desk-rejected. The contribution must be stated explicitly in the introduction: not "this paper examines..." but "this paper contributes to tourism theory by..."

2. **Methodological sophistication**: TM has become increasingly methods-driven. Reviewers expect state-of-the-art methods. A paper using OLS regression without addressing endogeneity, or basic thematic analysis without methodological depth, will draw criticism.

3. **Managerial/policy implications with SAR specificity**: Reviewers demand implications that are specific and actionable for destination managers or policymakers. Generic recommendations ("managers should improve service quality") are a red flag. Use the SAR framework: Setting (which destination/context) → Analytical Findings (what the data revealed) → Recommendations (specific actions). Number the implications.

4. **Global relevance**: TM is a general tourism journal, not a regional one. Single-country or single-destination studies must demonstrate relevance beyond that context. "While this study is situated in Barcelona, the findings regarding overtourism management strategies are applicable to mature urban destinations worldwide experiencing similar pressures."

5. **Engagement with TM literature**: Reviewers check whether you have cited recent TM papers on your topic. A paper on destination image that cites only JTR and ATR papers — with no TM citations — signals poor positioning.

**Red Flags for TM Reviewers**:
- "The theoretical contribution is unclear or overstated"
- "The methods are outdated compared to current standards in the field"
- "This paper could have been published in a generic marketing/management journal with minimal changes — where is the tourism-specific contribution?"
- "The managerial implications are generic and could apply to any service industry"
- "The literature review focuses on generic theory without engaging the tourism-specific literature"

**Typical TM Scorecard**:
- Overall: Reject / Major Revision / Minor Revision / Accept
- Originality and contribution: 1 (weak) to 5 (outstanding)
- Relationship to literature: 1 to 5
- Methodology: 1 to 5
- Results and interpretation: 1 to 5
- Implications for tourism management: 1 to 5
- Quality of communication: 1 to 5

### 2.2 Annals of Tourism Research (ATR)

**Publisher**: Elsevier
**Impact Factor**: ~10.0 (2024)
**Acceptance Rate**: ~8-12%
**Typical Review Rounds**: 2-4

**What Reviewers REALLY Look For**:

1. **Theoretical depth over empirical contribution**: ATR is THE theory journal in tourism. It prioritizes theoretical development, conceptual innovation, and critical perspectives. A paper with an elegant theory and adequate methods can succeed at ATR; a paper with brilliant methods and thin theory will struggle.

2. **Social science breadth**: ATR draws on anthropology, sociology, geography, psychology, economics, and political science. Reviewers value interdisciplinary theoretical engagement. "We draw on Goffman's (1959) dramaturgical framework from sociology to reconceptualize the tourist encounter as a staged performance" — this kind of theoretical import is welcomed at ATR.

3. **Critical and reflexive positioning**: ATR reviewers appreciate reflexivity and critical engagement with the phenomenon under study. Do not simply describe tourism — interrogate it. "While destination marketing constructs an image of authenticity for tourist consumption, our findings reveal how this construction simultaneously erases indigenous voices and histories."

4. **Conceptual papers are welcome**: Unlike TM, ATR explicitly welcomes conceptual papers that develop new theoretical frameworks, challenge existing paradigms, or synthesize disparate literatures. The conceptual paper must still be systematic and convincing — not an unstructured essay.

5. **Methodological pluralism**: ATR publishes the full range of methods, from advanced econometrics to autoethnography. Reviewers evaluate methods within their own paradigm. A qualitative paper is not judged by quantitative standards, and vice versa. BUT: the methods must be rigorous within their paradigm.

6. **Writing quality**: ATR papers are expected to be well-written, with a strong narrative arc. Poor writing — unclear argument, repetitive structure, awkward prose — is a common rejection reason even for theoretically strong papers.

**Red Flags for ATR Reviewers**:
- "This paper is descriptive rather than theoretical — it reports findings without advancing tourism theory"
- "The theoretical framework is superficial — name-dropping theorists without genuine engagement with their ideas"
- "The paper makes claims about 'theory development' but merely applies an existing framework to a new context"
- "Where is the critical engagement? The paper takes industry and tourist perspectives at face value"
- "The contribution is incremental — the paper confirms what we already know rather than challenging or extending existing knowledge"

**ATR-Specific Submission Types**:
- **Research articles**: Full empirical or conceptual papers (8,000-10,000 words)
- **Research notes**: Short, focused contributions — a single crisp finding or conceptual insight (3,000 words max)
- **Commentaries**: Responses to published ATR articles
- **Curated collections**: Thematic special issues

### 2.3 Journal of Travel Research (JTR)

**Publisher**: SAGE
**Impact Factor**: ~10.0 (2024)
**Acceptance Rate**: ~10-15%
**Typical Review Rounds**: 2-3

**What Reviewers REALLY Look For**:

1. **Methodological rigor is the primary currency**: JTR reviewers are methodologically demanding. They expect state-of-the-art quantitative and/or qualitative methods, thoroughly justified and correctly executed. If using SEM, they expect comprehensive measurement model assessment. If using qualitative methods, they expect deep methodological engagement (not just "we did thematic analysis").

2. **Travel research focus**: JTR has a narrower scope than TM or ATR — it focuses on TRAVEL (the movement, the experience, the industry) rather than tourism broadly. The connection to travel behavior, travel industry, or travel experience must be clear. A paper on hotel employee turnover is marginal unless connected to the travel experience.

3. **Practical relevance**: JTR values research that can inform travel industry practice. Managerial implications should be specific to travel/tourism practitioners — not generic advice. JTR has historically had a stronger applied orientation than ATR.

4. **Quantitative methods preferred but not exclusive**: JTR publishes more quantitative than qualitative research, but excellent qualitative papers are welcomed. The key is methodological rigor within the chosen paradigm.

5. **Data quality matters**: JTR reviewers scrutinize sampling, response rates, and data quality. Convenience samples (e.g., MTurk, student samples) without strong justification will be criticized. "Data were collected from Amazon MTurk workers" requires: justification for why MTurk is appropriate for this tourism study, attention check reporting, and acknowledgment of representativeness limitations.

**Red Flags for JTR Reviewers**:
- "The sample is not representative of the tourist population claimed"
- "Common method bias is a serious concern given the cross-sectional self-report design, and the paper's treatment of CMB is insufficient"
- "The measurement model is inadequately assessed — AVE, discriminant validity, and alternative model comparisons are needed"
- "The contribution to travel research is unclear — how does this advance our understanding of travel behavior specifically?"
- "Generalizability is overstated given the single-destination convenience sample"

**JTR-Specific Requirements**:
- **Data availability statement**: JTR requires authors to state whether data are available and how they can be accessed
- **Methodological appendix**: Detailed methods often go to an online appendix
- **Practical implications section**: Often explicitly required in the manuscript structure

### 2.4 International Journal of Hospitality Management (IJHM)

**Publisher**: Elsevier
**Impact Factor**: ~9.0 (2024)
**Acceptance Rate**: ~10-15%
**Typical Review Rounds**: 2-3

**What Reviewers REALLY Look For**:

1. **Hospitality focus**: IJHM is the premier HOSPITALITY journal. Papers must be clearly situated in hospitality contexts — hotels, restaurants, events, foodservice, cruise lines, etc. A paper on destination image is marginal at IJHM unless the study focuses on hotel image or restaurant image. "Tourism" papers that lack a hospitality hook are better suited for TM, ATR, or JTR.

2. **Managerial relevance**: IJHM values research that hospitality managers can USE. Conceptual contributions are welcome but must connect to hospitality management practice. The "So what?" for a hotel GM or restaurant manager must be clear and compelling.

3. **Methodological expectations**: IJHM expects rigorous methods but is somewhat more accepting of applied research designs than TM or JTR. Case studies with managerial insights, well-designed surveys with practical implications, and experimental studies are all welcome. The threshold for methodological innovation is lower, but the threshold for correct application of standard methods is the same.

4. **Industry connection**: IJHM reviewers appreciate papers that demonstrate connection to the hospitality industry — data from real hotels/restaurants, collaboration with industry partners, or implications validated by industry stakeholders.

5. **Timeliness**: IJHM values research on current hospitality challenges — technology adoption (AI, robots), sustainability in hospitality, COVID-19 recovery, labor shortages, platform economy (Airbnb, food delivery apps).

**Red Flags for IJHM Reviewers**:
- "This is a tourism paper, not a hospitality paper — it should be submitted to a tourism journal"
- "The managerial implications are too generic for hospitality practitioners"
- "The hospitality context is incidental — the same study could have been conducted in any service industry"
- "The paper does not engage with the hospitality-specific literature on this topic"

**IJHM Sections**:
- Research papers (full empirical studies)
- Research notes (shorter, focused contributions)
- Review papers (comprehensive literature reviews — only by invitation or with editor approval)
- Special issue papers

### 2.5 International Journal of Contemporary Hospitality Management (IJCHM)

**Publisher**: Emerald
**Impact Factor**: ~8.0 (2024)
**Acceptance Rate**: ~10-18%
**Typical Review Rounds**: 2-3

**What Reviewers REALLY Look For**:

1. **Contemporary relevance**: IJCHM prioritizes research on CONTEMPORARY (current, emerging) hospitality issues. COVID-19, AI/robotics, sustainability, diversity and inclusion, the sharing economy, wellness hospitality, digital transformation, crisis management. A paper on a well-worn topic (e.g., basic service quality-satisfaction-loyalty links) faces an uphill battle unless it offers a genuinely novel perspective.

2. **Rigorous and innovative**: IJCHM has elevated its methodological standards significantly in recent years. It now competes with IJHM for top hospitality papers. Reviewers expect rigorous methods and clear contributions. Purely descriptive studies without theoretical grounding are unlikely to succeed.

3. **Global perspective**: IJCHM values international and cross-cultural perspectives. Single-country studies must articulate their broader relevance. Comparative studies (cross-country, cross-cultural) are particularly welcomed.

4. **Engagement with practice**: Similar to IJHM, IJCHM expects clear, actionable implications. But IJCHM also values papers that critically examine industry practice — problematizing taken-for-granted assumptions in hospitality management.

5. **Special issues**: IJCHM publishes many special issues. Papers in special issues must meet the same standards as regular issues. "Special issue paper" is not a lower bar.

**Red Flags for IJCHM Reviewers**:
- "The topic is not contemporary — this issue has been studied extensively for decades without a new angle"
- "The paper lacks theoretical grounding — it is descriptive rather than theoretically informed"
- "The contribution is unclear beyond the specific context studied"

### 2.6 Journal of Sustainable Tourism (JST)

**Publisher**: Taylor & Francis
**Impact Factor**: ~7.0 (2024)
**Acceptance Rate**: ~15-20%
**Typical Review Rounds**: 2-4

**What Reviewers REALLY Look For**:

1. **Sustainability is the core, not an add-on**: JST reviewers are sustainability specialists. Sustainability must be the central focus — a paper on destination image with one question about environmental attitudes appended is NOT a JST paper. The sustainability lens must be integral to the theoretical framework, research questions, and analysis.

2. **Critical perspective on sustainability**: JST reviewers appreciate critical engagement with sustainability concepts — not taking "sustainable tourism" at face value as an unproblematic good. "While the resort markets itself as 'eco-friendly,' our analysis reveals that its sustainability practices are performative — designed for guest satisfaction rather than genuine environmental protection."

3. **Triple bottom line**: JST expects engagement with environmental, social, AND economic dimensions of sustainability. Papers focusing on only one dimension must acknowledge the others.

4. **Stakeholder perspectives**: JST values research that considers multiple stakeholder perspectives — tourists, residents, industry, government, NGOs. Single-stakeholder studies are acceptable but must acknowledge the limitations of that perspective.

5. **Transformative agenda**: JST has an implicit (sometimes explicit) transformative agenda — research should contribute to making tourism more sustainable, not just describe unsustainable practices. Implications should suggest pathways toward sustainability transformation.

**Red Flags for JST Reviewers**:
- "Sustainability is not the central focus — this paper could be published in a general tourism journal"
- "The sustainability framing is superficial — the paper uses sustainability as a buzzword without substantive engagement"
- "The paper ignores the social/economic/environmental dimensions of sustainability"
- "The recommendations do not address the systemic barriers to sustainability transformation"
- "Greenwashing: the paper presents incremental changes as sustainability without engaging with the scale of transformation needed"

**JST-Specific Expectations**:
- Clear definition of which sustainability paradigm is adopted (weak vs. strong sustainability, sustainable development vs. degrowth)
- Engagement with SDGs is common but must be substantive, not tokenistic
- Climate change implications should be addressed where relevant

## 3. The Review Report Structure

Understanding how tourism reviewers write their reports helps you preempt their concerns.

### Typical Tourism Review Structure

```
1. Summary (1 paragraph)
   - Restate what the paper examines
   - Overall assessment (positive/negative/mixed)
   - Recommendation: Accept / Minor Revision / Major Revision / Reject

2. Major Comments (3-6 items)
   - Theoretical contribution concerns
   - Methodology issues
   - Results interpretation problems
   - Missing or misrepresented literature
   - Structural/organizational issues

3. Minor Comments (5-15 items)
   - Specific clarification requests
   - Additional analyses requested
   - Presentation and formatting issues
   - Missing citations
   - Typographical errors
```

### Common Reviewer Phrases (and What They Mean)

| Reviewer Says | Actually Means |
|---------------|---------------|
| "The theoretical contribution is unclear" | You haven't articulated what is NEW about your theory/knowledge contribution. You described what you did, not what we learn from it. |
| "The theoretical contribution is incremental" | The gap between your paper and existing literature is too small. Adding one variable to an established model is not enough. |
| "The paper lacks a coherent theoretical framework" | Your hypotheses are ad hoc rather than derived from theory. You listed constructs but didn't explain WHY they should be related. |
| "The methods section is insufficiently detailed" | A reviewer cannot replicate your study from your description. Missing: sampling procedure, response rate, measurement details, analysis steps. |
| "Common method bias is a concern" | You used a single cross-sectional self-report survey without procedural or statistical CMB remedies. |
| "The sample is not representative" | Your convenience sample (MTurk, students, single hotel) cannot support the population-level claims you make. |
| "The implications are too generic" | "Managers should improve service quality" is meaningless. What specifically should they do, based on your specific findings? |
| "The paper does not fit this journal" | Wrong topic-methodology-contribution profile for this venue. See journal-specific expectations above. |
| "The literature review is outdated" | You've missed major publications from the last 3-5 years, especially from the target journal. |
| "The hypotheses are not adequately justified" | Each hypothesis needs a theoretical rationale, not just "Previous research has found that X affects Y, so we hypothesize H1: X affects Y." This is tautological. |
| "The discussion does not engage with the literature" | You restated your results without comparing them to prior findings. Which findings support prior work? Which contradict it? Why? |
| "More engagement with tourism literature is needed" | You cited generic psychology/marketing/management sources but not the specific tourism literature on your topic. |

## 4. Preempting Common Criticisms

### 4.1 "The theoretical contribution is unclear/incremental"

**Preemption Strategy**:
1. State your contribution in one sentence in the introduction. Use a contribution template: "This study contributes to [theoretical domain] by [specific contribution], addressing the gap that prior research has [what was studied] but has not [what was missing]."
2. Build a **gap table** (see Writing Patterns Reference) mapping your contribution against 8-12 key prior studies across 5-7 dimensions.
3. Distinguish between types of theoretical contribution: (a) new construct introduction, (b) new relationship identified, (c) boundary condition established, (d) mechanism explained, (e) competing theories tested, (f) context-specific theory developed. State which type YOU contribute.
4. In the Discussion, explicitly state: "Our findings extend theory by showing that..." and "Contrary to [existing theory], we find that..."

### 4.2 "Common method bias is a concern"

**Preemption Strategy**:
1. Apply at least TWO procedural remedies ex ante (Section 3.1 of measurement-scales reference).
2. Apply at least TWO statistical diagnostics ex post (Section 3.2 of measurement-scales reference).
3. Report all remedies and diagnostics transparently in the Method section AND Limitations.
4. Acknowledge: "Despite these remedies, CMB cannot be entirely ruled out in a cross-sectional survey design. Future research should employ longitudinal or multi-source designs to further address this concern."
5. If feasible, collect criterion data from a different source (e.g., actual hotel occupancy data rather than self-reported revisit intention).

### 4.3 "The sample is not representative"

**Preemption Strategy**:
1. Describe the sampling frame, sampling method, and response rate in detail.
2. Compare your sample demographics to population demographics (e.g., compare to official tourism statistics for that destination/country). If your sample differs from the population, acknowledge this.
3. Use careful language: "This study's findings are based on a sample of [description] and may not generalize to [population not represented]. However, the relationships tested are theoretically motivated and should be examined in other contexts before wider generalization."
4. If using MTurk or online panels: cite studies showing these samples can produce valid results for theory-testing (not point estimation). Use attention checks and report the proportion of respondents who passed.
5. For student samples: justify why students are APPROPRIATE for THIS study (e.g., "University students represent a key segment of the youth tourism market, which accounts for 23% of international tourist arrivals globally.")

### 4.4 "The implications are too generic"

**Preemption Strategy (SAR Framework)**:

For each managerial implication:
- **Setting**: Specify the context. "In mature urban destinations facing overtourism pressure..."
- **Analytical Finding**: What did your data reveal? "Our results show that tourists who perceive crowding are 34% less likely to express revisit intentions, but this effect is attenuated by 50% when tourists perceive effective visitor management."
- **Recommendation**: What should managers DO? "Destination managers should implement AND communicate visitor management strategies (e.g., timed entry, dynamic pricing, dispersal marketing). Our findings suggest that tourists' PERCEPTION that crowding is being managed matters as much as actual crowd reduction."

Number your implications: "Managerial Implication 1," "Managerial Implication 2," etc.

### 4.5 "More engagement with tourism literature is needed"

**Preemption Strategy**:
1. Ensure your literature review devotes at least 40% of citations to TOURISM journals (TM, ATR, JTR, IJHM, IJCHM, JST, CIT, JTR, etc.), not just generic social science journals.
2. Cite recent (last 3 years) papers from your target journal on your topic. Reviewers notice when you have not cited THEIR journal.
3. Use the tourism literature to MOTIVATE your study, not just as an afterthought. "While the marketing literature has established the satisfaction-loyalty link (Oliver, 1999), tourism research reveals that this relationship is contingent on destination-specific factors such as cultural distance (Chen & Tsai, 2007) and novelty-seeking (Assaker & Hallak, 2013)."

## 5. Rebuttal Strategies

### 5.1 Structure of a Rebuttal Letter

```
Dear Editor,

We thank the reviewers for their careful reading and constructive
feedback. We have revised the manuscript to address all comments.
Below, we respond to each point. Reviewer comments are in italics;
our responses follow.

[Point-by-point responses organized by reviewer]

We believe the revised manuscript is substantially strengthened
and hope the reviewers agree.

Sincerely,
The Authors
```

### 5.2 Rebuttal Principles

1. **Be gracious**: Thank the reviewer for EVERY comment, even (especially) harsh ones. NEVER argue, dismiss, or respond defensively.

2. **Be specific and verifiable**: "We have added [specific content] to page [X], paragraph [Y], which reads: '[quoted text].'" Never say "We have addressed this."
   - Bad: "We have clarified the sampling procedure."
   - Good: "We have revised the sampling description on p. 12, para 2: 'Participants were recruited through a systematic sampling procedure at the departure gate of Barcelona-El Prat Airport, with every 5th passenger approached. Of 1,200 passengers approached, 847 agreed to participate (response rate: 70.6%).'"

3. **Show the change**: Quote the added or modified text in your response. This makes it easy for the reviewer to evaluate the revision without re-reading the entire manuscript.

4. **Pick your battles strategically**: If a reviewer asks for an infeasible analysis (e.g., collecting new data), explain why it's infeasible AND propose an alternative.
   - "We appreciate the reviewer's suggestion to collect longitudinal data to establish causality. Unfortunately, this is beyond the scope of the current revision. However, we have (a) added a more extensive discussion of this limitation on p. 28, (b) included a post-hoc test of reverse causality using [alternative approach], and (c) noted this as an important direction for future research."

5. **If you respectfully disagree, provide evidence**: "We respectfully note that [alternative evidence or interpretation]. However, we appreciate the reviewer's perspective and have added a discussion of this alternative interpretation on p. X."

6. **Track changes**: Submit a marked-up version showing all changes (track changes or color-coded). Also submit a clean version. Make it trivially easy for reviewers to see what changed.

### 5.3 When a Reviewer Is Wrong

If a reviewer makes a factual error or misinterprets your findings:
1. **NEVER** say "The reviewer is wrong" or "The reviewer misunderstood."
2. **INSTEAD**: "We appreciate the reviewer raising this point. To clarify, [gentle correction with evidence]. We have revised the text on p. X to prevent this misunderstanding: '[revised text].'"
3. If the reviewer's criticism is based on a different methodological paradigm (e.g., a quantitative reviewer criticizing a qualitative study using quantitative standards), politely explain your paradigmatic stance: "We appreciate the reviewer's concern about generalizability. Following the qualitative research tradition, our study seeks transferability (Lincoln & Guba, 1985) rather than statistical generalization. We have clarified this distinction and provided thicker description (pp. 14-16) to enable readers to assess transferability to their contexts."

### 5.4 The "Impossible Revision" Request

Occasionally, a reviewer requests an analysis that is genuinely impossible with your data. Options:
1. **Explain feasibility constraints**: "We acknowledge that [requested analysis] would strengthen causal inference. However, our data were collected as part of a cross-sectional survey, making this analysis infeasible. We have added this as a limitation and suggested it as a direction for future research."
2. **Propose an alternative**: "While we cannot conduct [requested analysis] with the current data, we have added [alternative analysis] that partially addresses the reviewer's concern."
3. **Acknowledge and move on**: If the request is unreasonable, politely acknowledge it without committing: "We appreciate this suggestion and note that it represents an important direction for future research."

## 6. Self-Review Checklist (Before Submission)

### Theoretical Contribution
- [ ] Can I state the single main theoretical contribution in one sentence?
- [ ] Would a tourism scholar in my subfield say "I wish I had thought of that"?
- [ ] Is the contribution explicitly labeled in the introduction (not just implied)?
- [ ] Does the Discussion explain how my findings extend, challenge, or refine existing theory?
- [ ] Have I distinguished my contribution from that of the closest prior studies?

### Methodological Rigor
- [ ] Is the sampling procedure described in sufficient detail for replication?
- [ ] Are all measures properly cited and their psychometric properties reported (alpha, AVE, CR)?
- [ ] Have I addressed common method bias with both procedural and statistical remedies?
- [ ] Have I reported ALL necessary fit indices (chi-square/df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA with 90% CI, SRMR)?
- [ ] Are all statistical assumptions tested (normality, homoskedasticity, multicollinearity, etc.)?
- [ ] For qualitative studies: have I addressed credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability (Lincoln & Guba)?

### Tourism Relevance
- [ ] Would a tourism/hospitality academic AND practitioner care about this paper?
- [ ] Have I cited the tourism-specific literature on my topic (not just generic social science)?
- [ ] Do my implications use the SAR framework (Setting → Analytical Finding → Recommendation)?
- [ ] Are at least 40% of my references from tourism journals?

### Positioning
- [ ] Have I cited at least 3-5 recent papers from the target journal?
- [ ] Is my literature review organized thematically, not paper-by-paper?
- [ ] Does my gap table clearly demonstrate where my contribution fits?
- [ ] Have I explained why my paper belongs in THIS journal (not just any tourism journal)?

### Presentation
- [ ] Is the abstract within the journal's word limit and correctly structured?
- [ ] Are all figures and tables referenced in the text?
- [ ] Are all citations verified (author names, years, journal names correct)?
- [ ] Is the reference list formatted to the journal's style guide?
- [ ] Have I run a spell-check and grammar check?
- [ ] Have I removed all identifying information (if double-blind review)?
- [ ] Is the manuscript length within the journal's guidelines?
