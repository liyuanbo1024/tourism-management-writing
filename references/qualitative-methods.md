# Qualitative Methods in Tourism Research

This reference covers the major qualitative research traditions used in top-tier tourism journals. Each section addresses philosophical foundations, procedural guidance, trustworthiness, reporting standards, and common pitfalls.

---

## 1. Grounded Theory

Grounded theory is the most frequently cited qualitative methodology in tourism research. It is used to generate theory inductively from data, particularly for understanding under-researched tourism phenomena.

### 1.1 Philosophical Variants

**Glaser and Strauss (1967) — Classic/Objectivist Grounded Theory**

- **Epistemological stance**: Post-positivist. Assumes an objective reality that emerges from the data.
- **Role of literature**: Literature review is conducted AFTER data collection and analysis to avoid contaminating emergent theory with preconceived concepts. In practice, tourism journals require a preliminary literature review for the proposal/introduction.
- **Coding process**: Substantive coding (open and selective) → Theoretical coding (identifying relationships between categories using coding families: the "Six Cs" — causes, consequences, covariances, contingencies, conditions, contexts).
- **Core category**: The central phenomenon around which all other categories are integrated. Glaser insists that the core category "emerges" rather than being chosen.
- **Theoretical memos**: Ongoing writing that captures analytical thinking. Memos are the bridge between coding and the final write-up. Glaser: "If you skip the memoing, you are not doing grounded theory."
- **Distinguishing feature**: Emergence. The researcher must trust that theory will emerge from constant comparison.

**Strauss and Corbin (1990, 1998, 2008) — Systematic/Straussian Grounded Theory**

- **Epistemological stance**: Pragmatist. Acknowledges researcher interpretation but provides systematic procedures.
- **Role of literature**: Literature can be used early as a source of "theoretical sensitivity" — helping the researcher recognize what is theoretically significant in the data.
- **Coding process**: Open coding (breaking data into discrete parts, identifying concepts) → Axial coding (reassembling data around a central category using the paradigm model: causal conditions → phenomenon → context → intervening conditions → action/interaction strategies → consequences) → Selective coding (integrating categories around the core category).
- **Conditional/consequential matrix**: A diagram linking micro-level actions to macro-level structural conditions. A hallmark of the Strauss-Corbin approach. Tourism researchers sometimes struggle to apply this meaningfully.
- **Distinguishing feature**: Structure. The paradigm model provides scaffolding that makes the method accessible but risks forcing data into predetermined categories.

**Charmaz (2006, 2014) — Constructivist Grounded Theory**

- **Epistemological stance**: Constructivist/Interpretivist. Rejects the objectivist assumption that theory "emerges" from data. Instead, theory is constructed through the interaction between researcher and participants.
- **Role of literature**: Acknowledged as part of the researcher's interpretive framework. Use literature to sensitize, not to prescribe. A preliminary review is acceptable, but concepts from extant theory are treated as "sensitizing concepts" — starting points for inquiry, not hypotheses to be tested.
- **Coding process**: Initial coding (word-by-word, line-by-line, incident-by-incident, using gerunds — "feeling trapped" not "trapped") → Focused coding (using the most significant/frequent initial codes to sift through large amounts of data) → Theoretical coding (specifying possible relationships between categories).
- **Memo-writing**: Emphasized as a reflexive practice. Charmaz treats memo-writing as the pivotal intermediate step between data collection and writing drafts.
- **Theoretical sampling**: Sampling guided by emerging theory to elaborate and refine categories. Not convenience sampling.
- **Distinguishing feature**: Reflexivity and constructivism. Charmaz foregrounds the researcher's role in co-constructing the analysis.

### 1.2 Which version should be used and stated?

Tourism papers MUST specify which version of grounded theory is being used and cite the appropriate methodological text. Reviewer comment: "The paper claims to use grounded theory but does not specify which variant. Are you following Glaser, Strauss & Corbin, or Charmaz? The procedures differ substantially."

### 1.3 Procedural Guide for Tourism Applications

**Data Collection in Tourism:**

- **Interviews**: Semi-structured interviews with tourists, hotel managers, destination marketers, policy makers. Typically 20-40 interviews, but the actual number is determined by theoretical saturation, NOT by a predetermined quota.
- **Participant observation**: The researcher observes and participates in tourism activities (e.g., guided tours, hotel check-in, festival attendance).
- **Documents**: Destination marketing materials, travel blogs, TripAdvisor reviews, government tourism policy documents, hotel brochures.
- **Multi-source**: Combining interviews with tourists AND service providers AND documents. This triangulation is highly valued in tourism grounded theory studies.

**Theoretical Saturation**:

This is the most contested and most misunderstood concept in tourism grounded theory studies.

- **Glaser (1978)**: Saturation is reached when no NEW properties of the core category emerge. It is NOT when you have stopped finding new data instances — you always find new instances. It is about the theoretical category, not the data.
- **Charmaz (2014)**: Saturation is when gathering fresh data no longer sparks new theoretical insights, nor reveals new properties of core theoretical categories.
- **Reporting**: Tourism papers must explicitly state: (a) how saturation was determined (e.g., "Theoretical saturation of the core category was reached after 28 interviews, at which point three additional interviews confirmed that no new properties were emerging"); (b) the final sample size; (c) the point at which the researcher judged saturation was achieved.

**Saturation pitfalls in tourism contexts**:
- Interviewing only Western tourists and claiming saturation for "tourist experience" (ignoring cultural variation)
- Interviewing only during peak season and claiming saturation (ignoring seasonal variation in tourist types)
- Sampling only at 5-star hotels and claiming saturation for "hotel service quality" (ignoring segment variation)
- Sampling only online and claiming saturation (ignoring non-online tourists, such as older or less tech-savvy travelers)

### 1.4 Reporting Standards

**Essential Elements**:
1. Statement of grounded theory variant (Glaserian, Straussian, or Charmaz constructivist) with citation
2. Description of coding process with examples (e.g., "Initial open coding of the first eight interviews generated 347 open codes, which were subsequently grouped into 42 categories through constant comparison. Axial coding, guided by the paradigm model, identified 12 subcategories aggregated under 5 main categories.")
3. Evidence of theoretical sampling ("Based on the emerging category of 'authenticity seeking,' we subsequently sampled tourists who had booked heritage tours...")
4. Evidence of constant comparison (ideally, include a memo excerpt or a code-to-category table)
5. The core category clearly identified and defined
6. A theoretical model (diagram) showing relationships between categories
7. Excerpts from interviews linked to theoretical categories (not just illustrative quotes in isolation)

### 1.5 Common Mistakes

1. **Grounded theory "lite"**: Performing basic thematic coding and calling it grounded theory. Grounded theory requires: theoretical sampling, constant comparison, theoretical saturation, and memo-writing — not just open coding.
2. **Premature closure**: Claiming saturation after 12 interviews when categories are still thin and underdeveloped.
3. **Ritualistic citation**: Citing Glaser & Strauss (1967) but following none of their procedures. Reviewers will catch this.
4. **Confusing grounded theory with content analysis**: Content analysis counts and categorizes manifest content. Grounded theory develops abstract theoretical categories through constant comparison.
5. **Failing to distinguish description from theory**: A grounded theory paper should present a theoretical framework, not just a descriptive summary of themes.
6. **Interview-only grounded theory**: True grounded theory benefits from multiple data sources. Tourism papers that use only interviews miss the opportunity to observe tourist behavior, analyze promotional materials, or examine policy documents.

## 2. Phenomenology

Phenomenology studies the lived experience of a phenomenon — what it is like to be a tourist, to experience a destination, to undergo a transformative travel event.

### 2.1 Variants in Tourism Research

**Descriptive Phenomenology (Husserl)**

- **Goal**: Describe the invariant structure (the "essence") of a lived experience, bracketing presuppositions.
- **Epoché (bracketing)**: The researcher sets aside their preconceptions about the phenomenon. In tourism, this means bracketing your own travel experiences, your academic knowledge of tourism concepts, and your cultural assumptions.
- **Procedure**: (1) Epoché — bracketing; (2) Phenomenological reduction — returning to the experience as it is given; (3) Imaginative variation — varying the phenomenon in imagination to identify its essential features; (4) Synthesis — describing the essential structure.
- **Weakness in tourism**: True bracketing is philosophically contested. How can a tourism researcher bracket their knowledge of tourism theory? Many tourism phenomenologists acknowledge partial bracketing at best.

**Interpretive Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) (Smith, Flowers, and Larkin 2009)**

- **Goal**: Explore in detail how participants make sense of their personal and social world. IPA is idiographic (focuses on the particular, not the general) and committed to the detailed examination of individual cases before moving to cross-case analysis.
- **Theoretical foundations**: Phenomenology (the study of experience), hermeneutics (the theory of interpretation), and idiography (the study of the particular).
- **Double hermeneutic**: The researcher is making sense of the participant making sense of their experience. This is a core IPA concept that must be acknowledged.
- **Sample**: Small, purposive, homogeneous (typically 3-15 participants). Homogeneity is important — IPA seeks depth in a specific group, not breadth across diverse groups. A tourism IPA paper studying the experience of solo female backpackers in South Asia would recruit specifically solo female backpackers who traveled in South Asia, not tourists generally.
- **Procedure**:
  1. Reading and re-reading: Immersion in the first transcript
  2. Initial noting: Descriptive comments (content), linguistic comments (language use), conceptual comments (interrogative, interpretive)
  3. Developing emergent themes: Reducing the volume of notes to concise, interpretive themes
  4. Searching for connections: Clustering emergent themes into superordinate themes
  5. Moving to the next case: Bracketing themes from the first case and treating the next case on its own terms
  6. Looking for patterns across cases: Which superordinate themes are shared? Which are idiosyncratic?

**IPA Reporting in Tourism**:
- Present superordinate themes with subordinate themes nested within them
- Each theme must be illustrated with extracts from participants, followed by analytical interpretation (not just description)
- Include a table of themes with illustrative quotes
- Present convergence and divergence (shared themes and individual variations)
- Include a reflexive account of the researcher's position

**Common Mistakes in Tourism Phenomenology**:
1. Applying IPA to samples that are too large (N > 15) — IPA is idiographic; large samples sacrifice depth
2. Providing descriptive summaries of participant accounts without interpretive analysis
3. Treating IPA as a method to generate theory (it is a method for exploring experience, though some phenomenological findings can contribute to theory)
4. Neglecting the double hermeneutic — not acknowledging the researcher's interpretive role
5. Using a heterogeneous sample (e.g., "tourists in Thailand" without specifying type, nationality, travel purpose) — IPA requires homogeneity

### 2.2 Distinguishing Grounded Theory from Phenomenology

| Dimension | Grounded Theory | Phenomenology |
|-----------|----------------|---------------|
| Purpose | Generate theory | Describe lived experience |
| Focus | Social processes, action, interaction | The meaning and essence of experience |
| Outcome | A theoretical framework/model | The structure of an experience |
| Sample size | 20-40 (typically larger) | 3-15 (IPA is explicitly small) |
| Literature | Delayed (varies by variant) | Used to frame the phenomenon |
| Analysis unit | Incidents, actions, processes | Lived experience, meaning-making |

## 3. Ethnography and Netnography in Tourism

### 3.1 Ethnography

**Definition**: The study of people in their natural settings, involving prolonged engagement, participant observation, and the production of "thick description" (Geertz 1973).

**Tourism-Specific Considerations**:

1. **The challenge of "the field"**: Tourism is inherently mobile and transient. The traditional ethnographic assumption of a bounded field site is complicated when tourists are constantly moving, encounters are brief, and the "destination" is produced through the interaction of tourists, locals, and industry actors.

2. **Multi-sited ethnography** (Marcus 1995): Following the tourist across multiple sites. A study of backpacker culture might follow tourists from a hostel in Bangkok to a beach in Koh Phangan to a border crossing into Cambodia. This captures the mobile, networked nature of contemporary tourism.

3. **Access and ethics in tourism settings**:
   - Resort/hotel ethnography requires gatekeeper permission (management)
   - Tour group ethnography requires both guide and participant consent
   - Festival ethnography involves public spaces but requires sensitivity about photographing and recording
   - Slum tourism, dark tourism, and volunteer tourism raise acute ethical questions about exploitation, voyeurism, and power

4. **Insider/Outsider positionality**: Tourism ethnographers must navigate multiple identities — researcher, tourist, guest, stranger, customer. Reflexive accounts of how these identities shaped data collection are essential.

**Key Elements to Report**:
- Duration of fieldwork (total days/weeks — prolonged engagement is a trustworthiness criterion)
- Description of the field site(s)
- Role of the researcher (complete participant, participant-as-observer, observer-as-participant, complete observer — Gold 1958)
- Gatekeeper relationships and access negotiation
- Data collection methods (field notes, interviews, documents, photographs/video)
- Reflexivity: how the researcher's presence and identity shaped the data

**Thick Description (Geertz 1973)**:
Tourism ethnography must provide context-rich descriptions that allow the reader to understand not just what tourists do, but what their actions mean within the specific cultural context. A wink is not just a contraction of the eyelid — it is a coded communication. Similarly, a tourist photographing a sunset is not just operating a camera — it is performing "the tourist gaze" (Urry 1990).

### 3.2 Netnography (Kozinets 2010, 2015)

**Definition**: A qualitative research methodology that adapts ethnographic techniques to the study of online communities and cultures. It is particularly suited to tourism, given the centrality of TripAdvisor, Instagram, travel blogs, YouTube travel vlogs, Reddit travel forums, and Facebook travel groups.

**Netnographic Procedure (Kozinets 2015)**:

1. **Entrée**: Identifying and gaining access to the online community. In tourism, this might be a TripAdvisor forum destination community, a Facebook group for solo female travelers, or a subreddit (r/travel, r/solotravel, r/digitalnomad). Gatekeeper permission varies by platform — overt vs. covert research must be justified.

2. **Data collection**:
   - **Archival data**: Pre-existing online communications that the researcher copies directly (forum posts, reviews, blog entries, Instagram captions)
   - **Elicited data**: Data co-created through researcher interaction (interviews via private message, responses to researcher posts)
   - **Fieldnote data**: The researcher's reflective notes on community dynamics, member interactions, and their own experiences
   - **Visual data**: Photographs, videos, memes, emoji usage

3. **Analysis and interpretation**: Iterative hermeneutic circle — moving between the specific data fragments and the emerging understanding of the online cultural phenomenon.

4. **Research ethics**:
   - **Public vs. private distinction**: Are the posts publicly accessible without login? Do community members perceive the space as private? On TripAdvisor — public. In a closed Facebook group — private. This distinction determines whether informed consent is required from each member (private) or whether the researcher can use publicly posted data (with anonymization).
   - **Anonymization**: Use pseudonyms. Do NOT quote verbatim phrases that can be searched online to identify the original poster. This is a unique netnographic challenge — direct quotes are traceable via search engines.
   - **Copyright**: Data posted online is copyrighted by the creator. Fair use/dealing provisions may apply for research purposes.

**Types of Netnography in Tourism**:
1. **Symbolic netnography**: Studying online representations of tourism — how destinations construct their image on Instagram, how hotels respond to negative reviews on TripAdvisor
2. **Digital netnography**: Studying the online dimensions of tourist behavior — how tourists use smartphones during travel, how they construct digital travel narratives
3. **Auto-netnography**: The researcher studies their own online tourist practices

**Netnographic Reporting**:
> We conducted a netnography of the r/solotravel subreddit community (2.3 million members as of January 2024). After obtaining moderator permission, we collected 1,247 posts and 8,432 comments from January to June 2024. Following Kozinets (2015), we employed an iterative hermeneutic analysis process, moving between specific data fragments and emerging understanding of the solo travel culture. All usernames were replaced with pseudonyms, and direct quotes were altered sufficiently to prevent search-engine identification while preserving meaning.

## 4. Case Study Design

Case study research is one of the most commonly claimed yet poorly executed methodologies in tourism research. Many papers claim to be case studies without following established case study methodology.

### 4.1 Yin (2018) Framework

Yin's case study methodology is the dominant approach cited in tourism journals.

**Definition**: "A case study is an empirical inquiry that investigates a contemporary phenomenon in depth and within its real-world context, especially when the boundaries between phenomenon and context may not be clearly evident" (Yin 2018, p. 15). This definition captures why case study is appropriate for tourism: destination competitiveness, crisis management, and destination governance are phenomena that cannot be separated from their context.

**When Case Study Is Appropriate** (Yin 2018):
1. The research question is a "how" or "why" question
2. The researcher has little or no control over behavioral events
3. The focus is on a contemporary (not historical) phenomenon

**Design Typology**:

| | Single Case | Multiple Cases |
|---|---|---|
| **Holistic** (single unit of analysis) | Type 1: One destination, one unit (e.g., the governance structure of Destination X) | Type 3: Multiple destinations, one unit each (e.g., governance structures of Destinations X, Y, Z) |
| **Embedded** (multiple units of analysis) | Type 2: One destination, multiple units (e.g., hotels, attractions, and transport within Destination X) | Type 4: Multiple destinations, multiple units each (e.g., within-destination subsystems across multiple destinations) |

**Single Case Rationales**:
1. **Critical case**: The case tests a well-formulated theory (e.g., testing Butler's TALC model on a destination that precisely matches its assumptions)
2. **Extreme or unusual case**: The case deviates from norms (e.g., a destination that recovered from a major crisis against all expectations)
3. **Revelatory case**: The researcher has access to a case previously inaccessible (e.g., a newly opened country, a closed resort with unique access)
4. **Longitudinal case**: The same case studied at multiple points in time

Explicitly state which rationale applies to your case. Do not claim a single case study without justification.

**Components of Case Study Design** (Yin 2018):
1. **Research questions**: "How" or "why" (e.g., "How did Destination X recover its tourism arrivals after the 2015 earthquake?")
2. **Propositions**: Tentative answers that guide data collection (e.g., "Destination X recovered through a combination of proactive crisis communication, public-private partnership, and leveraging loyal repeat visitors")
3. **Unit of analysis**: Clearly defined case boundaries (e.g., "The case is the destination management organization's crisis response during January 2015–December 2017")
4. **Logic linking data to propositions**: Pattern matching, explanation building, time-series analysis, logic models
5. **Criteria for interpreting findings**: How will you know if your proposition is supported?

**Sources of Evidence** (use at least three for triangulation):
- Documentation (policy documents, annual reports, marketing plans)
- Archival records (visitor statistics, budget data)
- Interviews (semi-structured with key informants)
- Direct observation (site visits)
- Participant observation (attending DMO meetings, industry events)
- Physical artifacts (marketing collateral, wayfinding signage)

**Case Study Protocol**: A formal document that guides data collection. It should include:
- Overview of the case study project
- Data collection procedures
- Data collection questions (distinct from research questions — these are the questions the researcher asks of the data)
- Guide for the case study report outline

### 4.2 Alternative Case Study Approaches

**Stake (1995) — Interpretive/Intrinsic Case Study**:
- The case is chosen because of intrinsic interest in that particular case, not because it represents other cases or illustrates a theoretical proposition. Used when studying a unique tourism phenomenon.
- Emphasis on "naturalistic generalization" — the reader, not the researcher, generalizes from the case through vicarious experience.

**Eisenhardt (1989) — Theory Building from Cases**:
- Uses multiple cases (typically 4-10) for theory building
- Cross-case analysis using tables and graphs to identify patterns
- Theoretical sampling based on theoretical categories, not random selection
- Highly structured, systematic approach. Popular in management and increasingly in tourism entrepreneurship.

### 4.3 Reporting Standards for Tourism Case Studies

1. **Rationale for the case**: Why was this destination/hotel/event chosen? State the single-case rationale (Yin) or the sampling logic (Eisenhardt).
2. **Case description**: Provide sufficient context — location, history, scale, key actors. The reader must understand the case to judge transferability.
3. **Methods of data collection**: List all sources. Tourism case studies that rely solely on interviews (without documents, observation, or secondary data) are methodologically weak.
4. **Analytical strategy**: State whether you used pattern matching, explanation building, time-series analysis, or cross-case synthesis.
5. **Chain of evidence**: The reader should be able to trace from research questions → data sources → evidence → findings → conclusions. Citation to specific data sources throughout the findings.
6. **Triangulation**: Describe how findings were corroborated across multiple sources.
7. **Case study database**: Maintain an organized repository of all evidence (separate from the final report) available for inspection.

### 4.4 Common Mistakes

1. **"Case study" as a convenient label**: Calling any study of a single destination or hotel a "case study" without following case study methodology. This is the #1 reviewer complaint.
2. **Inadequate case description**: The reader cannot assess the transferability of findings without knowing what the case is.
3. **No triangulation**: Single-source case studies (interviews only) lack the methodological rigor that distinguishes case study from other interview-based methods.
4. **Overgeneralization**: Drawing sweeping conclusions from a single, non-critical case. "This study of one eco-lodge in Costa Rica reveals that eco-tourism..." — No.
5. **Neglecting rival explanations**: Strong case studies actively seek evidence that would disconfirm the propositions, rather than only seeking confirming evidence.

## 5. Content Analysis

### 5.1 Thematic Analysis (Braun and Clarke 2006, 2019)

Thematic analysis is the most widely used qualitative analytic method in tourism — and the most superficially applied.

**Braun and Clarke's Six Phases**:

1. **Familiarizing yourself with the data**: Repeated reading, noting initial observations. In tourism, transcribe interviews verbatim. Read and re-read.
2. **Generating initial codes**: Systematic coding across the entire dataset. Code at both semantic (surface) and latent (underlying) levels.
3. **Searching for themes**: Collating codes into potential themes. A theme captures something important about the data in relation to the research question. It is NOT a topic summary. "Service quality" is a topic. "The tension between standardization and personalization in luxury hotel service" is a theme.
4. **Reviewing themes**: Checking if themes work in relation to coded extracts (Level 1) and the entire dataset (Level 2). Create a thematic map.
5. **Defining and naming themes**: Writing a detailed analysis of each theme. Identify the "essence" of each theme — what is the unifying concept?
6. **Producing the report**: Writing the analytical narrative with vivid, compelling extract examples that illustrate the analytic points, not just describe the data.

**Reflexive Thematic Analysis (Braun and Clarke 2019)**:
The updated approach emphasizes that themes do not "emerge" from data — they are actively generated by the researcher through their interpretive engagement. The researcher's subjectivity is a resource, not a bias to be eliminated.

**Reporting in Tourism**:
> We employed reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke (2006, 2019). Analysis was conducted iteratively throughout the data collection period (May–August 2023). After familiarization through repeated reading, we generated initial semantic and latent codes across the 24 interview transcripts. Themes were developed through iterative review and refinement, with attention to both convergence and divergence within the dataset. We present four themes, each illustrated with participant extracts and our interpretive analysis.

### 5.2 Directed Content Analysis (Hsieh and Shannon 2005)

**Purpose**: Validating or extending an existing theoretical framework in a new tourism context. Coding is guided by predetermined categories from theory, but the researcher remains open to new categories that emerge.

**Procedure**:
1. Identify key concepts from existing theory as initial coding categories
2. Provide operational definitions for each category (e.g., "Authenticity, following Wang (1999): objective authenticity of toured objects, constructive authenticity projected onto objects by tourists, existential authenticity activated by tourist activities")
3. Code all text that appears to represent each category
4. Text that cannot be categorized with the initial scheme is given a new code
5. Report both confirmatory and newly emergent findings

### 5.3 Computer-Assisted Qualitative Data Analysis Software (CAQDAS)

- **NVivo**: Dominant in tourism. Supports coding, memoing, queries, visualization (word trees, cluster analysis, concept maps). Note: using NVivo does not make the analysis more rigorous; it organizes data but does not analyze it.
- **ATLAS.ti**: Stronger for multi-media data (tourist photos, videos).
- **MAXQDA**: Strong mixed methods integration.
- **Dedoose**: Browser-based, collaborative.

**Reporting CAQDAS use**: "Data were managed using NVivo 12, which facilitated systematic coding, retrieval of coded segments, and the construction of a thematic framework. The analysis itself, however, was conducted by the researchers through iterative engagement with the data, not automated by the software." This distinction — software manages, researcher analyzes — is important to reviewers.

## 6. Trustworthiness Criteria (Lincoln and Guba 1985)

Lincoln and Guba (1985) proposed parallel criteria to replace reliability and validity for qualitative research. These are the standard framework cited in tourism qualitative research.

### 6.1 The Four Criteria

**1. Credibility (parallel to internal validity)**

The confidence in the truth of the findings. For tourism research: Do the findings accurately represent the tourist experience / destination management practice / stakeholder perspective?

**Techniques to Establish Credibility**:
- **Prolonged engagement**: Sufficient time in the field to understand the culture and context. For tourism ethnography: sufficient immersion to move beyond "tourist gaze" observations to deeper cultural understanding. Minimum: several weeks to months.
- **Persistent observation**: Focused, in-depth observation of the most salient elements. Not just "being there," but systematically observing specific phenomena.
- **Triangulation**: Of methods (interviews + observation + documents), sources (tourists + managers + residents), investigators (multiple researchers coding independently), and theories (applying alternative theoretical lenses).
- **Member checking**: Returning findings to participants for verification. In tourism, this is often done through follow-up interviews or summary presentations to hotel/destination managers. Report how member checks were conducted and participant feedback. Note: tourists may be unreachable after their trip; plan member checking during data collection or plan for manager-only member checks.
- **Peer debriefing**: Engaging a disinterested peer to review the analysis and challenge assumptions. Report who the debriefer was and their role.
- **Negative case analysis**: Actively seeking and analyzing cases that do not fit the emerging pattern. "Of the 24 tourists interviewed, 22 expressed satisfaction with their experience; however, two participants reported significant disappointment. We analyze these negative cases to refine and qualify our understanding of tourist satisfaction."

**2. Transferability (parallel to external validity)**

The extent to which findings can be transferred to other contexts. In qualitative research, transferability is the responsibility of the reader, not the researcher, but the researcher must provide sufficient detail for the reader to make this judgment.

**Technique**: **Thick description** — provide rich, detailed descriptions of the research context, participants, and findings. The reader should be able to determine: "Does this apply to my destination / hotel / tourist segment?"

**Minimum Description for Tourism Studies**:
- The destination (location, type, scale, development stage)
- The participants (demographics, travel experience, nationality, travel purpose)
- The temporal context (season, year, any ongoing events affecting tourism)
- The researcher's relationship to the context (insider/outsider, gatekeeper access)

**3. Dependability (parallel to reliability)**

The consistency of the findings — would the same findings be reached if the study were repeated with similar participants in a similar context?

**Technique**: **Inquiry audit** — an external auditor examines the research process and product. In practice, tourism PhD theses may have a formal audit trail; published papers more commonly use:
- **Audit trail**: Detailed documentation of research decisions, including raw data, analysis notes, codebooks, theme development records, and methodological decisions
- **Stepwise replication**: Multiple researchers independently analyze the same data and compare findings (report inter-coder agreement: percentage agreement AND Cohen's kappa ≥ 0.70)
- **Code-recoding**: The primary coder recodes the data after a time interval and compares with original coding

**4. Confirmability (parallel to objectivity)**

The extent to which findings are shaped by the respondents and not researcher bias, motivation, or interest.

**Techniques**:
- **Audit trail** (same as for dependability — serves both criteria)
- **Reflexive journal**: The researcher's ongoing documentation of personal reflections, methodological decisions, and analytical insights. Distinguish between the reflexive journal (personal reflections) and the methodological log (procedural decisions).
- **Confirmability audit**: An external auditor examines whether findings, interpretations, and conclusions are supported by the data

### 6.2 Reporting Trustworthiness

Do NOT write: "Trustworthiness was ensured through credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability." This is vacuous. Instead:

> Credibility was established through three strategies: (1) prolonged engagement — the first author spent 14 weeks at the destination, participating in daily tourism activities and building rapport with key informants; (2) triangulation — interview data were cross-referenced with field observations, destination marketing materials, and TripAdvisor reviews (N=340); (3) member checking — a two-page summary of preliminary findings was emailed to 10 key informants representing hotels, tour operators, and the DMO; their feedback was incorporated into the final analysis. Dependability was addressed through an audit trail maintained in NVivo, documenting the coding process from initial open codes (N=487) through theme development to final thematic structure.

## 7. Reporting Standards: COREQ for Interviews

The **COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research (COREQ)** checklist (Tong, Sainsbury, and Craig 2007) is the standard reporting framework for interview-based qualitative studies. Top tourism journals increasingly require or recommend it.

**Three Domains (32 items)**:

**Domain 1: Research Team and Reflexivity** (8 items)
- Personal characteristics: interviewer credentials, occupation, gender, experience/training
- Relationship with participants: prior relationship established, participant knowledge of interviewer, interviewer characteristics reported

**Domain 2: Study Design** (15 items)
- Theoretical framework: methodological orientation stated (e.g., grounded theory, phenomenology)
- Participant selection: sampling method, method of approach, sample size, non-participation rate
- Setting: setting of data collection, presence of non-participants, description of sample
- Data collection: interview guide, repeat interviews, audio/visual recording, field notes, duration, data saturation, transcripts returned

**Domain 3: Analysis and Findings** (9 items)
- Data analysis: number of data coders, description of coding tree, derivation of themes, software, participant checking
- Reporting: quotations presented (with participant identifiers), data and findings consistent, clarity of major themes, clarity of minor themes

**How to Use COREQ in Tourism Papers**:
- Complete the COREQ checklist and submit as supplementary material
- In the methods section, ensure all COREQ items are addressed
- Even if the journal does not explicitly require COREQ, addressing its items preempts reviewer concerns about methodological transparency

## 8. Cross-Cutting Qualitative Issues in Tourism

### 8.1 Language and Translation

Tourism research frequently involves cross-cultural, multilingual data collection. Translation is an interpretive act, not a mechanical procedure.

**Best Practices**:
1. **State the languages involved**: interviews in Language A, analysis in Language B, publication in Language C
2. **Describe the translation process**: forward translation (Language A → Language B) AND back-translation (Language B → Language A) to verify fidelity. Report who did the translation (bilingual researcher, professional translator) and their qualifications.
3. **Translation during analysis**: If analysis is conducted on translated transcripts, the original-language transcripts should be consulted for key quotes and ambiguous passages.
4. **Quotes**: Present quotes in the original language with English translation. "We present participant quotes in the original [language] followed by English translation in brackets."

### 8.2 Positionality and Reflexivity

Tourism researchers are often — implicitly or explicitly — tourists themselves, and many study destinations that are economically dependent on tourism. This creates unique positionality challenges.

**Required Reflexive Statements**:
> As a researcher who had visited the destination as a tourist five years prior, I entered the field with pre-existing impressions of the destination. Throughout the research, I maintained a reflexive journal to document how my prior experiences shaped my interpretations. My position as a Western researcher in a developing-country tourism context required ongoing attention to power dynamics in interviews with local tourism workers, where my presence as a foreign researcher may have influenced responses toward socially desirable accounts of their working conditions.

### 8.3 Saturation vs. Information Power

Malterud, Siersma, and Guassora (2016) proposed "information power" as an alternative to saturation. Information power depends on: (a) the aim of the study (broad aims require more participants), (b) sample specificity (dense, specific samples require fewer participants), (c) use of established theory (theory-driven studies need fewer participants), (d) quality of dialogue (richer interviews yield more information per participant), (e) analysis strategy (cross-case analysis needs more participants than in-depth case analysis). This framework offers a more nuanced alternative to claiming "saturation" as a black box.
