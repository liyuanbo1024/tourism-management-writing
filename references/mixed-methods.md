# Mixed Methods in Tourism Research

Mixed methods research combines quantitative and qualitative approaches to address research questions that neither approach alone can adequately answer. In tourism, mixed methods is particularly valuable because tourism phenomena have both measurable dimensions (arrivals, spending, satisfaction scores) and experiential, interpretive dimensions (meaning-making, emotional responses, cultural encounters).

---

## 1. When Mixed Methods Is Preferred vs. Single Method

### 1.1 Justifications for Mixed Methods in Tourism

A mixed methods study must justify why BOTH approaches are needed. The most common justification in tourism — "to get a richer understanding" — is insufficient. Use one of the following specific justifications:

**1. Complementarity**: Quantitative and qualitative methods address different aspects of the same phenomenon. *Tourism example*: A survey measures the relationship between destination image and satisfaction (quantitative); interviews explore how tourists construct and negotiate destination image during their trip (qualitative). Neither can substitute for the other.

**2. Development**: Results from one method inform the other method. *Tourism example*: Qualitative interviews with hotel managers identify relevant dimensions of service innovation → These dimensions are operationalized as items in a survey administered to a larger sample of hotels.

**3. Initiation**: Contradictions between quantitative and qualitative findings prompt new insights. *Tourism example*: Survey results show high satisfaction scores, but interview participants express significant frustrations. The contradiction reveals that satisfaction scales may capture cognitive evaluation but miss emotional dimensions, prompting a reconceptualization of tourist satisfaction.

**4. Expansion**: Extending the breadth of the inquiry by using different methods for different components. *Tourism example*: Quantitative analysis of visitor statistics to map seasonality patterns → qualitative ethnography of tourist experiences during peak and off-peak seasons to understand how seasonality shapes the tourism experience.

**5. Triangulation**: Seeking convergence across methods to increase confidence in findings. *Tourism example*: Hotel occupancy data, manager interviews, and TripAdvisor reviews all indicate declining service quality during peak season → high confidence in this finding.

**6. Offsetting weaknesses**: Using each method to compensate for the limitations of the other. *Tourism example*: Survey measures behavioral intention (quantitative), but social desirability bias inflates stated intentions → interviews probe actual past behavior and the discrepancy between stated intention and actual behavior (qualitative).

### 1.2 When NOT to Use Mixed Methods

- **Forced fit**: The research question is adequately addressed by a single method. Adding a token qualitative component for "richness" is NOT mixed methods — it is a quantitative study with some quotes.
- **Insufficient expertise**: The researcher lacks competence in one of the methods. Reviewers detect this quickly — a paper with rigorous quantitative analysis and superficial qualitative work (or vice versa) will be criticized.
- **Resource constraints**: A high-quality single-method study is better than a low-quality mixed methods study. Mixed methods requires roughly twice the data collection and analysis resources.
- **Incommensurability**: The paradigms underlying the methods are fundamentally incompatible at the philosophical level. If you are a strict post-positivist (quantitative) or strict constructivist (qualitative), genuinely integrating methods from different paradigms requires explicit philosophical justification. Many mixed methods tourism papers gloss over this and are criticized for it.

### 1.3 Paradigm Stance

Mixed methods researchers must explicitly state their paradigmatic position. The main options:

| Paradigm | Stance | Implication for Tourism Research |
|-----------|--------|----------------------------------|
| **Pragmatism** (Morgan 2007) | "What works" — methods are tools chosen based on the research question, not philosophical allegiance. | Most common in tourism mixed methods. Adopt the method that best answers each part of the research question. |
| **Critical realism** (Bhaskar 1975) | An objective reality exists, but our access to it is mediated by social and linguistic constructions. Quantitative methods capture structural mechanisms; qualitative methods capture meaning-making. | Useful for explaining tourism phenomena at both structural (economic forces, policy) and experiential (tourist meaning-making) levels. |
| **Dialectical pluralism** (Johnson 2017) | Different paradigms are in dialogue — their differences are productive. | Rare in tourism but gaining ground. Acknowledges that quantitative and qualitative findings may contradict, and these contradictions are informative. |
| **Transformative** (Mertens 2007) | Research should advance social justice. Mixed methods serves to give voice to marginalized groups while also documenting structural inequalities statistically. | Relevant for tourism research on indigenous tourism, slum tourism, or gender in tourism employment. |

## 2. Mixed Methods Designs

### 2.1 Sequential Explanatory Design

**Structure**: QUAN → qual (quantitative phase first, followed by qualitative phase to explain quantitative results)

**Purpose**: The qualitative phase helps explain, interpret, or contextualize the quantitative findings. This is the most common mixed methods design in tourism.

**Tourism Example**:
> Phase 1 (QUAN): Survey of 500 tourists at a heritage destination measuring satisfaction, authenticity perception, and behavioral intention. Regression analysis reveals that authenticity perception is the strongest predictor of satisfaction but explains only 24% of variance.

> Phase 2 (qual): Semi-structured interviews with 20 tourists from the survey sample who scored high on authenticity perception but low on satisfaction (or vice versa) to understand the cases that deviate from the regression pattern. Interviews reveal that some tourists perceive "staged authenticity" negatively while others accept it positively, explaining the weak overall relationship.

**Key Procedural Considerations**:
- Phase 2 participants should be PURPOSIVELY sampled from Phase 1 participants based on Phase 1 results (not a convenience sample)
- The connection between phases must be explicit: "Phase 2 interviews explored WHY tourists who rated authenticity as high reported lower satisfaction — a pattern identified in Phase 1 regression analysis"
- Report sample selection criteria for Phase 2 with reference to Phase 1 results
- The qualitative phase is prioritized — it explains the quantitative results. Title: "An Explanatory Sequential Mixed Methods Study of..."

**Reporting Challenges**:
- The paper cannot report all Phase 1 results first then all Phase 2 results — this creates an awkward structure. Better: present relevant Phase 1 results, then Phase 2 findings, integrated within thematic subsections.
- Avoid treating the qualitative phase as secondary. Reviewers will criticize tokenistic qualitative work that merely "adds quotes" to statistical findings.

### 2.2 Sequential Exploratory Design

**Structure**: QUAL → quan (qualitative phase first, followed by quantitative phase to test/generalize qualitative findings)

**Purpose**: The qualitative phase explores a phenomenon and generates hypotheses, constructs, or instruments; the quantitative phase tests these with a larger sample.

**Tourism Example**:
> Phase 1 (QUAL): Grounded theory study with 30 tourists exploring the phenomenon of "transformative travel." Three core dimensions emerge: disorientation, reflection, and reintegration.

> Phase 2 (quan): Scale development based on Phase 1 findings. Items generated from the qualitative themes, purified through expert review and pilot testing, and validated with a survey of 800 tourists. CFA confirms a three-factor structure. Structural model tests antecedents and consequences of transformative travel.

**Key Procedural Considerations**:
- Phase 1 findings MUST inform Phase 2 instrument development. Report how specific items were generated from specific qualitative themes (an item-to-theme mapping table is persuasive).
- Pilot test the new instrument before the main Phase 2 survey
- The quantitative phase is prioritized (the study's main contribution is the generalizable findings). However, the qualitative phase is essential — the constructs would not exist without it.

**Reporting**:
> "Based on the qualitative themes identified in Phase 1 (disorientation, reflection, reintegration), we generated an initial pool of 42 items for the Transformative Travel Scale. These were reviewed by a panel of five tourism academics for content validity, resulting in a refined pool of 28 items. Pilot testing with 120 tourists (Phase 2 pilot) yielded a 24-item scale after item analysis. The final survey (N=800) was used for CFA and structural model testing."

### 2.3 Convergent Parallel Design

**Structure**: QUAN + QUAL (quantitative and qualitative data collected concurrently, analyzed separately, then merged during interpretation)

**Purpose**: To obtain complementary data on the same phenomenon and combine the strengths of each method.

**Tourism Example**:
> QUAN: Survey of 400 tourists measuring destination image (cognitive, affective, conative dimensions).

> QUAL: Semi-structured interviews with 25 tourists exploring how they construct destination image before, during, and after travel.

> Integration: Comparing the statistically-derived image dimensions with the qualitatively-derived image construction processes. The survey reveals WHAT the image dimensions are; the interviews reveal HOW tourists construct, negotiate, and update these images.

**Merging Strategies**:
1. **Side-by-side comparison**: Present quantitative results in a column alongside qualitative themes
2. **Joint display**: A table or figure that directly integrates quantitative and qualitative findings (see Section 3)
3. **Data transformation**: Quantitize qualitative data (convert themes to frequencies) or qualitize quantitative data (narrative description of statistical findings)
4. **Narrative weaving**: Within each thematic section, present quantitative findings followed by qualitative illustration/expansion

**Key Procedural Considerations**:
- Equal priority is given to both methods (QUAN + QUAL, not QUAN + qual or quan + QUAL)
- The sample for qualitative interviews may be a subset of the survey sample (to enable direct comparison) OR a separate sample (to capture perspectives not accessible via survey)
- Analysis is conducted SEPARATELY — quantitative analysis is completed before seeing qualitative themes (to prevent contamination), and vice versa
- Integration occurs at the INTERPRETATION stage, not the analysis stage

**Common Mistake**: Analyzing quantitative data to find patterns, then selectively presenting qualitative quotes that confirm those patterns. True convergent design must be open to contradiction between the two datasets. Report divergent findings.

### 2.4 Embedded Design

**Structure**: QUAN(qual) or QUAL(quan) — a secondary method is embedded within a primarily quantitative or qualitative study.

**Purpose**: The embedded method addresses a different question than the primary method, supporting the overall research aim rather than providing a separate, equal contribution.

**Tourism Examples**:
> QUAN(qual): A randomized experiment testing the effect of two destination advertisement formats on booking intention (QUAN primary), with post-experiment interviews exploring participants' cognitive processing of the advertisements (qual embedded).

> QUAL(quan): An ethnography of Airbnb host-guest interactions (QUAL primary), with a short survey measuring host and guest demographic characteristics and satisfaction (quan embedded).

**Key Procedural Considerations**:
- The embedded method serves the primary method. Its role must be articulated.
- The embedded component may address a different research question from the primary study.
- Integration: how do the embedded findings enhance, explain, or contextualize the primary findings?

## 3. Integration

Integration is the defining feature of mixed methods research — and the most common point of failure. Many tourism studies claim mixed methods but keep quantitative and qualitative findings in separate sections, never truly integrating them.

### 3.1 Integration at the Design Level

Integration is planned from the start — the research design specifies how methods will be mixed.

**Design-Level Integration Decisions**:
- Will the qualitative and quantitative phases be sequential or concurrent?
- Will one method have priority, or will they have equal weighting?
- At what point will mixing occur — design, data collection, analysis, or interpretation?
- How will the sample relate across phases (same participants, subset, or independent samples)?

**Notation Convention** (Morse 1991):
- **UPPERCASE** = primary/dominant method (QUAN, QUAL)
- **lowercase** = secondary/supplementary method (quan, qual)
- **+** = concurrent
- **→** = sequential
- **( )** = embedded

Examples: QUAN → qual (sequential explanatory), QUAL → quan (sequential exploratory), QUAN + QUAL (convergent), QUAN(qual) (embedded)

### 3.2 Integration at the Methods Level

**Connecting**: One method's sample is linked to the other method's sample.
> "Interview participants were selected from survey respondents who scored in the top and bottom quartiles on the destination loyalty scale."

**Building**: One method's results inform the other method's data collection procedures.
> "Themes from Phase 1 interviews were used to develop survey items for Phase 2."

**Merging**: The two datasets are brought together for analysis and comparison.
> "We compared the statistical clusters of tourist segments with the qualitatively-derived tourist typology."

**Embedding**: One method is nested within the other's design.
> "Within the quasi-experiment (QUAN), we conducted think-aloud protocols (qual) to understand participants' decision processes."

### 3.3 Integration at the Interpretation Level

This is where most tourism mixed methods papers claim integration but fall short. True interpretive integration requires:

1. **Joint consideration**: Quantitative and qualitative findings are discussed together, not in separate "Quantitative Results" and "Qualitative Findings" sections.
2. **Comparison**: What do the two datasets agree on? What do they disagree on? Contradictions are analytically productive.
3. **Explanation**: How do qualitative findings explain quantitative patterns (or vice versa)?
4. **Meta-inferences**: Conclusions drawn from the integration that could not be drawn from either dataset alone.

**Organizing the Integrated Discussion**:

Rather than separating quantitative and qualitative results, organize by research question or theme:

> **RQ1: How does destination image influence tourist satisfaction?**
> 
> QUANTITATIVE FINDING: The regression model showed that affective image (β = 0.47, p < 0.01) was a stronger predictor of satisfaction than cognitive image (β = 0.21, p < 0.05).
> 
> QUALITATIVE FINDING: Interviews revealed that tourists' emotional responses to the destination (excitement, relaxation, wonder) dominated their narratives. Cognitive evaluations ("the infrastructure was good") were mentioned only when prompted.
> 
> INTEGRATED INSIGHT: The convergence of survey and interview data suggests that destination managers should prioritize affective positioning over purely informational marketing. However, the qualitative data also revealed that negative cognitive evaluations ("the beach was dirty") could override positive affect — a contingency not captured by the linear regression.

### 3.4 Joint Displays for Mixed Methods Results

A joint display is a table or figure that presents quantitative and qualitative results side by side, demonstrating how they are integrated. It is the single most effective device for demonstrating genuine mixed methods integration to reviewers.

**Joint Display Template**:

| Research Question | Quantitative Finding | Qualitative Finding | Integrated Interpretation |
|-------------------|---------------------|---------------------|---------------------------|
| RQ1: How does authenticity affect satisfaction? | Authenticity is a significant predictor (β = 0.34, p < 0.01) | Tourists described authenticity as "feeling like we saw the real place" | Both methods converge: perceived authenticity matters. However, interviews revealed two distinct types of authenticity — existential and objective — which the survey's unidimensional scale collapsed. Future research should differentiate these. |
| RQ2: What role does price play? | Price sensitivity is moderated by travel distance (β_interaction = -0.18, p < 0.05) | Long-haul tourists described price as "not the main concern"; short-haul tourists compared prices to local alternatives | Convergence: quantitative interaction effect is explained by qualitative price comparison frame. Long-haul tourists anchor to total trip cost; short-haul tourists anchor to local reference prices. |
| RQ3: Are tourists loyal? | 68% report high revisit intention | Only 3 of 25 interviewees had previously revisited; most expressed desire to visit "new places" | Divergence: high stated loyalty on surveys may reflect social desirability or momentary satisfaction rather than actual behavioral commitment. The "intention-behavior gap" in tourism loyalty merits further investigation. |

**Joint Display Principles**:
1. **Side-by-side presentation**: The reader should see quantitative and qualitative findings in the same visual field
2. **Integration column is essential**: The final column demonstrates integration and provides meta-inferences. This is the column that makes the paper a mixed methods paper, not two papers stapled together.
3. **Show convergence AND divergence**: A joint display that only shows convergence suggests confirmation bias. Include contradictions — they are analytically productive.
4. **Reference the joint display in text**: "Table 3 presents a joint display integrating the survey and interview findings. As shown, the quantitative and qualitative data converge on the importance of [X] but diverge on [Y], prompting a reconsideration of..."

## 4. Reporting Standards: GRAMMS Guidelines

The **Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS)** guidelines (O'Cathain, Murphy, and Nicholl 2008) provide a structured framework for reporting mixed methods research.

### GRAMMS Checklist

| # | Item | Tourism-Specific Guidance |
|---|------|--------------------------|
| 1 | Describe the justification for using a mixed methods approach | State one of the six specific justifications (Section 1.1), not the generic "for richer understanding" |
| 2 | Describe the design in terms of purpose, priority, and sequence | Use Morse notation: QUAN → qual, etc. State which method was prioritized. |
| 3 | Describe each method in terms of sampling, data collection, and analysis | Quantitative: sample size, sampling strategy, response rate, measures, analysis. Qualitative: sample size, sampling strategy, data collection procedure, analysis approach. |
| 4 | Describe where integration occurred | Design level, methods level, interpretation level, or all three. Be specific: "Integration occurred at the methods level (building: Phase 1 interview themes informed Phase 2 survey items) and at the interpretation level (merging through joint displays and narrative weaving)." |
| 5 | Describe the limitations of each method | Acknowledge that mixed methods does not eliminate limitations — it combines methods, each with their own limitations. |
| 6 | Describe insights gained from mixing methods | What did you learn from the integration that you would not have learned from either method alone? This is the essential GRAMMS question. If you cannot answer it, you do not have a genuine mixed methods study. |

### Additional Reporting Elements for Tourism

7. **Timeline**: Report when each phase was conducted. In tourism, seasonality matters. "Phase 1 (QUAL) was conducted during the peak season (July-August 2023), while Phase 2 (quan) was conducted during the shoulder season (October 2023). Seasonal differences in tourist profiles may affect comparability."

8. **Sample relationship**: Report how the samples relate across phases (same participants, subset, independent). This is critical for assessing integration validity.

9. **Integration validity**: Did the integrated findings hold up? Threats to integration validity include: (a) samples that do not represent the same population, (b) contradictory findings that are ignored rather than explored, (c) one method dominating interpretation so that the other method is mere decoration.

## 5. Quality Criteria for Tourism Mixed Methods

### 5.1 Mixed Methods-Specific Threats to Validity

**1. Sample integration problem**: The quantitative and qualitative samples may not be comparable. A survey of 500 tourists at an airport captures a different population than 20 in-depth interviews with tourists recruited through a tour operator. The inference that qualitative themes "explain" quantitative patterns may be invalid if the samples differ systematically.

**Solution**: Ensure sample comparability, or explicitly discuss how sample differences may affect integration. "While the survey sample comprised predominantly short-haul leisure tourists, the interview sample included both short-haul and long-haul tourists. This may explain the divergence in price sensitivity findings: long-haul interviewees expressed lower price sensitivity, which was not captured in the survey's aggregate results."

**2. Methodological weakness in one strand**: A well-executed quantitative analysis combined with a poorly executed qualitative analysis (or vice versa) undermines the entire study. Both strands must meet the quality standards of their respective traditions.

**3. Pseudo-integration**: Presenting quantitative and qualitative results in separate sections without meaningful integration. This is the single most common criticism of mixed methods papers in tourism. Evidence of pseudo-integration: the Discussion section does not reference any specific qualitative finding.

**4. Paradigm inconsistency**: Claiming a pragmatist stance but then treating qualitative findings as inferior "illustrations" of quantitative results. The methods must be treated as epistemologically commensurate, or the inequality must be explicitly justified.

**5. Insufficient data for one strand**: Adding a token qualitative component (2-3 interviews) to a quantitative study is not mixed methods. The qualitative strand must be sufficiently developed to stand on its own.

### 5.2 Reviewer Checklist for Mixed Methods

When reviewing your own mixed methods paper before submission:

- [ ] Is the mixed methods justification specific and substantive (not "for richer understanding")?
- [ ] Is the design clearly described with notation (QUAN → qual, etc.)?
- [ ] Is the integration demonstrated through joint displays and narrative weaving?
- [ ] Do the meta-inferences go beyond what either method alone would show?
- [ ] Are contradictions between methods acknowledged and explored?
- [ ] Are the limitations of each method discussed, including their interaction?
- [ ] Is the sample relationship across phases clearly described?
- [ ] Does the Discussion section cite specific qualitative AND quantitative findings?
- [ ] Is the GRAMMS checklist submitted as supplementary material?
- [ ] If one method is dominant, is this explicitly stated and justified?

## 6. Exemplary Tourism Mixed Methods Studies (to Emulate)

### Archetypal Designs in Tourism

| Design | Tourism Application | Key Feature |
|--------|---------------------|-------------|
| QUAN → qual | Survey identifies tourist satisfaction predictors → Interviews explain why cultural tourists report lower satisfaction despite overall high scores | Purposive sampling from survey for interviews |
| QUAL → quan | Grounded theory of volunteer tourism motivations → Survey tests the emergent motivation typology on a national sample | Item generation traceable to qualitative themes |
| QUAN + QUAL | Visitor survey + participant observation + manager interviews on destination carrying capacity | Joint display of visitor capacity scores and experiential descriptions |
| QUAN(qual) | Experiment on dynamic pricing for hotel rooms (QUAN) with embedded think-aloud protocols (qual) | Embedded design: think-alouds explain decision processes, serving the experiment |

## 7. Common Reviewer Criticisms and Preemption

| Reviewer Criticism | Preemption Strategy |
|-------------------|---------------------|
| "This is a quantitative study with some quotes, not mixed methods." | Explicitly state the mixed methods design (with notation); demonstrate integration through joint displays; show how qualitative findings contributed insights beyond illustration. |
| "The qualitative component is underdeveloped." | Provide sufficient detail on qualitative sampling, data collection, and analysis. Report sample size justification (saturation, information power). Include audit trail. |
| "The findings are not truly integrated." | Use joint displays. Organize Discussion by theme, not by method. Write meta-inferences. |
| "Why was mixed methods necessary?" | Provide one of the six specific justifications. Show what the integrated findings add beyond what either method alone contributed. |
| "The samples are not comparable." | Describe and justify the sample relationship across phases. If samples differ, discuss how this affects integration. |
| "One method dominates; the other is superficial." | Ensure both strands meet disciplinary quality standards. If one strand is supplementary, state this explicitly with justification. |
